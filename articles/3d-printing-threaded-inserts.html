How to Use Threaded Inserts in 3D Prints (Step-by-Step Guide)

📅 May 22, 2026 ⏱️ 8 min read ✍️ 3D Print Starter Team
🔩

You designed a bracket, printed it, and drove a screw right into the plastic. Two assemblies later, the threads are stripped and the screw spins freely. Sound familiar?

That's the fundamental problem with 3D printed threads: plastic can't handle repeated screw cycles. The solution is threaded inserts — small brass cylinders with internal threads that you melt into your print. They create metal threads that last thousands of cycles.

Why Threaded Inserts Beat Printed Threads

Assembly Cycles2–5 before stripping1000+
Clamping ForceLow (plastic deforms)High (metal-on-metal)

CostFree$0.05–0.20 per insert

For anything you'll assemble and disassemble more than once, inserts are the clear winner. They're standard in professional 3D printed assemblies.

Types of Threaded Inserts

Heat-Set Inserts (Most Common)

Brass cylinders with knurled exteriors and internal threads. You press them in with a heated soldering iron — the plastic melts around the knurling and locks the insert in place when it cools.

Best for: PLA, PETG, ABS — any thermoplastic that melts under 300°C. Not suitable for resin prints (thermoset, won't re-melt).

Press-Fit Inserts

Designed to be pressed in cold with an arbor press or vice. Barbs grip the plastic mechanically without heat.

Best for: Softer plastics, thin walls where heat might deform the part, or resin prints.

Ultrasonic Inserts

Installed with an ultrasonic welder that vibrates the insert, melting the surrounding plastic instantly. Professional-grade, not practical for home users.

Best for: Production environments only.

Tools You Need

  • Soldering iron (any basic one works, 30W+ recommended)
  • Insert tip (a conical brass tip that fits the insert — ~$8 on Amazon, or use the flat side of a standard tip)
  • Brass threaded inserts (M2, M3, M4, or M5 — M3 is most common)
  • Needle-nose pliers (to hold the insert while heating)
  • Your 3D printed part with correctly sized holes

Step-by-Step Installation

Step 1: Design the Hole

When designing your model, create a hole for the insert that's slightly smaller than the insert's outer diameter. The plastic needs to melt and flow into the knurling.

Recommended hole diameters (for standard heat-set inserts):

Insert SizeOuter DiameterHole DiameterMin Wall Thickness
M23.5mm3.0mm5mm
M34.6mm4.0mm6mm
M46.0mm5.2mm8mm
M57.0mm6.2mm9mm

Key rule: The hole should be 0.8–1.0mm smaller than the insert's outer diameter. This gives enough material to melt and grip.

Step 2: Print the Part

Print with at least 3 perimeters (walls) around the insert hole for maximum strength. Use your standard print settings — no special changes needed. Just make sure the holes are clean and circular.

Step 3: Heat the Insert

Set your soldering iron to 200–220°C (PLA) or 230–250°C (PETG/ABS). Place the insert over the hole, flat side down. Gently press the heated tip onto the insert.

Step 4: Press Into the Hole

Apply steady, straight downward pressure. The insert will sink into the plastic as it melts. Keep going until the top of the insert is flush with (or slightly below) the surface.

Step 5: Let It Cool

Hold the insert in place for 5–10 seconds while the plastic solidifies. Don't wiggle it. The plastic shrinking around the knurling is what locks it in.

Step 6: Test the Thread

Screw in a bolt. It should thread smoothly with firm resistance. If it spins freely, the insert wasn't held straight while cooling — reheat and realign.

Common Mistakes

  • Melting too deep: Stop when the insert is flush. Going too deep buries the threads below the surface and the bolt won't reach.
  • Tilted insert: If the insert goes in crooked, the bolt will cross-thread. Reheat, pull out, and redo.
  • Wrong hole size: Too small = insert won't fit, plastic bulges. Too large = insert spins freely. Always test with a scrap print first.
  • Too much heat: If the surrounding plastic deforms excessively, lower the iron temperature by 10°C.
  • Not enough wall thickness: Walls thinner than 3mm around the insert will crack. Add more perimeters or increase the part's thickness.

Best Insert Sizes for 3D Printing

M3 is the gold standard for 3D printed assemblies. It's small enough for compact parts, strong enough for most applications, and hardware (screws, bolts) is available everywhere.

  • M2: Tiny electronics mounts, small hinges
  • M3: General-purpose (brackets, enclosures, camera mounts)
  • M4: Medium-duty (clamps, jigs, tool holders)
  • M5: Heavy-duty (machine frames, structural joints)

Alternatives to Threaded Inserts

  • Self-tapping screws: Quick and dirty. Fine for one-time assembly. Strips after 3–5 cycles.
  • Nut pockets: Design a hex-shaped pocket and press a standard nut in from the side. Free and strong, but takes more design effort.
  • Captive nuts: Pause the print at a certain layer, drop in a nut, resume. The plastic prints around the nut. Works well for functional prints.

Where to Buy Threaded Inserts

  • Amazon: Search "M3 heat set inserts" — 100-pack for ~$8
  • AliExpress: Even cheaper, 200-pack for ~$6 (slower shipping)
  • McMaster-Carr: Industrial-grade, ultra-precise, ~$0.15 each

Buy an assortment pack (M2+M3+M4+M5) for ~$15 and you'll have inserts for every project.

The Bottom Line

Threaded inserts transform 3D printed parts from fragile prototypes into real functional assemblies. For $0.10 per insert and 30 seconds of work with a soldering iron, you get metal threads that survive thousands of cycles. If you're building anything that screws together, use them.
