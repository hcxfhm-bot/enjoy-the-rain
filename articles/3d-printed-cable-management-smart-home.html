3D Printed Cable Management for Smart Home Setups: 8 Practical Designs

📅 July 3, 2026 ⏱️ 11 min read ✍️ 3D Print Starter Team
🔌

You've built the dream smart home. ESP32 sensors in every room, Raspberry Pi running Home Assistant, smart switches, cameras, and a wall-mounted dashboard. It works beautifully — until you open the cabinet behind your TV or peek under your desk.

Cable chaos. Dangling USB cables, power bricks piled on the floor, Ethernet cables snaking everywhere. A smart home generates more wires than almost any other DIY hobby, and without a plan, it becomes a fire hazard and a maintenance nightmare.

The good news: your 3D printer is the perfect tool to tame this mess. Off-the-shelf cable management is either too generic or doesn't fit your specific devices. Custom 3D printed clips, channels, and organizers let you build exactly what your setup needs.

In this guide, I'll show you 8 practical 3D printed cable management designs that I use in my own smart home. Every one is printable on a basic Ender 3 or Bambu A1 Mini, requires no supports, and will make your installation look professional.

Why 3D Printed Cable Management Beats Store-Bought

Before we dive into the designs, let's talk about why printing your own makes sense:

  • Exact fit for your devices. A printed cable clip can be sized for the exact diameter of your USB-C cable, the exact spacing of your power strip, or the exact mounting holes of your ESP32 project.
  • Mount anywhere. Need to route cables along a specific wall or under a specific desk? Design the mount to use the screws or adhesive pads you already have.
  • Iterate and improve. Printed a clip that's too tight? Adjust the tolerance in your CAD file and print again. After 2-3 iterations, you have something perfect.
  • Cost per part is pennies. A pack of 50 adhesive cable clips costs $12 on Amazon. Printing 50 clips in PLA costs about $0.50 in filament.

Design 1: Adhesive-Backed Cable Clips (The Workhorse)

If you only print one cable management item, make it this. Adhesive-backed cable clips stick to walls, desks, or the back of entertainment centers. They hold individual cables in place without screws.

What to print: A small U-shaped clip with a flat back and a 3-8mm channel (depending on your cable diameter). Include a small lip so the cable snaps in and stays put. Print 10-20 at a time — they take 15 minutes each and use almost no filament.

Best filament: PLA for indoor use (easy to print, looks clean). PETG if the clips might get warm (behind a TV or near a router).

Pro tip: Use 3M VHB tape on the back instead of cheap adhesive pads. It holds forever, even on slightly textured walls.

Design 2: Under-Desk Cable Channel (The Clean Look)

If you have a standing desk or just want zero visible cables, print a cable channel that screws or adheres under your desk. All the wires run inside, and only the device cables exit at the exact spot they're needed.

What to print: A rectangular channel, open on one side, with mounting holes every 200mm. Channel width depends on your cable bundle — 30mm × 20mm fits most desk setups (2-3 power cables + 2-3 data cables). Print in 150-200mm sections and join them with small printed connectors.

Best filament: PETG. It's tougher than PLA and handles the occasional desk bump better. Black or white to match your desk.

Mounting: Use M3 × 8mm screws if your desk has pre-drilled holes, or 3M adhesive strips if not. Either way, position the channel so gravity keeps the cables inside when devices are unplugged.

Design 3: Power Strip Backplate (Hide the Brick Forest)

Smart homes need power — lots of it. USB chargers, smart plugs, Raspberry Pi power supplies, and router bricks create a "brick forest" behind every TV and desk. A backplate mounts to the wall and holds these bricks in place, with cutouts for each cable.

What to print: A flat plate with mounting holes, divided into cells sized for standard power bricks (roughly 60mm × 100mm × 30mm per cell). Each cell has a cable exit hole at the bottom. Screw the plate to the wall, zip-tie bricks into their cells, and suddenly your power setup looks intentional.

Best filament: PETG or ABS. These plates can get warm with multiple bricks plugged in.

Safety note: Don't fully enclose power bricks — they need airflow. The backplate just holds them in place; leave the front open or use a vented cover.

Design 4: ESP32/Arduino Sensor Mounts with Cable Routing

When you install DIY sensors around your house, you face two problems: where to put the sensor, and where to put the cable. A good mount solves both by integrating cable routing into the design.

What to print: A wall mount for your ESP32 or Arduino board that includes a built-in cable channel. The USB cable exits the back of the mount, runs through a printed channel, and pops out at a convenient spot for connection to a central USB hub or power supply.

Best filament: PLA for indoor sensors. PETG or ASA for outdoor or high-humidity locations (bathrooms, kitchens, outdoors).

Real-world example: I have an ESP32 with a BME280 temperature sensor mounted in my living room. The mount attaches to the wall with two screws, holds the ESP32 securely, and has a 200mm cable channel that routes the USB cable neatly to a nearby power outlet. No dangling wires, no adhesive — it looks like it came with the house.

Design 5: Cable Entry Grommets (Desk & Enclosure)

If you run cables through a desk or into an enclosure, you need a grommet. Store-bought grommets are either ugly or don't fit your cable bundle. A 3D printed grommet can be sized exactly for your needs.

What to print: A two-piece grommet: a base that inserts into a drilled hole, and a removable lid that clips on top. The lid has cutouts sized for your specific cables (one 8mm hole for Ethernet, one 5mm slot for USB, etc.). Print the base in the desk color, the lid in a contrasting color for a design accent.

Best filament: PLA for looks. Sand and paint if you want a perfect match to your desk.

Size guide: A 50mm diameter grommet fits most cable bundles (2-4 cables). Go up to 80mm if you're running HDMI, Ethernet, and power through the same hole.

Design 6: Smart Switch Mounting Brackets (No More Dangling Wires)

Smart switches and relays often end up dangling inside wall boxes or behind furniture. A mounting bracket secures them properly and routes the wires to avoid strain on the terminals.

What to print: A DIN-rail style mount or a custom bracket that fits your specific smart switch. Include strain relief features — small printed clips that hold the wires 20mm from the terminals so they can't pull tight and loosen the screw connections.

Best filament: PETG. It's flame-resistant enough for low-voltage applications and tough enough to handle occasional bumps during installation.

Important: For mains voltage devices, the printed bracket is only for mechanical support. All electrical connections must still meet your local electrical code. When in doubt, consult an electrician.

Design 7: Raspberry Pi / Home Assistant Server Rack (Mini)

If you run Raspberry Pi or other single-board computers as smart home hubs, they need a proper home. A mini "server rack" prints in a few hours and gives you a clean, organized way to mount 3-6 devices with all their cables routed properly.

What to print: A vertical rack with slots for Raspberry Pi boards (or other SBCs). Each slot has cutouts for the power cable, Ethernet cable, and any GPIO cables. The back of the rack has cable channels that route everything to a single exit point at the bottom. Include a spot to mount a small 5V or 12V power supply.

Best filament: PETG. The rack will be near electronics and may get warm. PETG's slightly flexible nature also helps with press-fit parts (like snapping Pi boards into their slots).

Ventilation: Don't fully enclose the rack. Print it with open sides or add vent holes so air can circulate around the Pis.

Design 8: Custom Cable Labels (Never Guess Again)

The final design isn't about routing cables — it's about identifying them. When you have 20 cables running to your smart home hub, you need to know which is which. 3D printed labels clip onto cables and identify them permanently.

What to print: Small tags (15mm × 40mm) with text embossed or debossed on the surface. The tag has a small channel on the back that snaps onto a cable. Print a set of tags labeled "LIVING RM TEMP", "OFFICE PIR", "KITCHEN RELAY", etc. Snap them onto the cables near your hub.

Best filament: PLA in a bright color (orange, yellow, or white). You want the labels to be visible against dark cables.

Text on prints: Use Tinkercad or Fusion 360 to add text directly to the 3D model. Don't use stickers or markers — they'll peel or fade within a year.

Filament Choice Cheat Sheet

Here's a quick reference for which filament to use for each cable management part:

Part Indoor (PLA) Outdoor/Humid (PETG/ASA)
Cable clips ✅ PLA PETG
Under-desk channel PETG (tougher) ASA (UV resistant)
Power brick backplate PETG or ABS ABS (heat resistant)
Sensor mounts ✅ PLA PETG or ASA
Raspberry Pi rack PETG N/A (indoor use)

Getting Started: Your First Printed Cable Management Part

If you're new to 3D printing cable management, start with Design 1 (adhesive-backed cable clips). They're the fastest print, use almost no filament, and you'll see an immediate improvement in your cable situation.

Here's a simple workflow:

  1. Measure your cable diameter with calipers or a ruler. Most USB cables are 3-4mm, Ethernet is 6mm, power cables are 5-8mm.
  2. Design a simple U-clip in Tinkercad (15 minutes for your first one). Make the channel 0.5mm larger than your cable diameter for a snap fit.
  3. Print 5 test clips in PLA. Check the fit — if the cable doesn't snap in, increase the channel diameter by 0.3mm and reprint.
  4. Once the fit is good, print 20-30 clips in one batch. They take 15-20 minutes each but print 4-6 at a time on a normal build plate.
  5. Install with 3M VHB tape and enjoy your organized cables.

Conclusion: A Tidy Smart Home is a Reliable Smart Home

Cable management isn't glamorous, but it's essential. A smart home with tangled cables is harder to troubleshoot, more likely to have connection issues, and definitely more dangerous if a wire gets pulled loose.

With a decent 3D printer and a spool of filament, you can print cable management that fits your exact setup. Start with simple clips and channels, then move on to custom mounts and labels as your skills improve.

Your future self — the one troubleshooting why the living room sensor went offline — will thank you for taking the time to organize things properly.
