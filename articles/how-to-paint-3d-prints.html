How to Paint 3D Prints: Sanding, Priming & Finishing

📅 May 13, 2026 ⏱️ 9 min read ✍️ 3D Print Starter Team
🎨

Why Paint Your 3D Prints?

A raw 3D print tells you what it is. A painted 3D print tells you what it could be. Layer lines disappear under primer. Plain plastic becomes weathered metal, aged wood, or glossy ceramic. The difference between "obviously 3D printed" and "did you really make that?" is almost always in the finishing.

Painting isn't just about aesthetics. A good paint job protects the print from UV damage, moisture, and handling wear. For cosplay props, painted pieces survive conventions far better than bare plastic. For gifts, a painted finish shows care and effort that raw filament can't match.

This guide covers the complete finishing workflow: sanding, priming, painting, and sealing. Follow these steps in order, and your prints will look professional.

Step 1: Sanding — The Foundation

Sanding is the most important step. No amount of paint will hide visible layer lines. You need a smooth surface before anything else.

Grit Progression

Start coarse and work your way fine:

  1. 120–220 grit: Remove major layer lines and print artifacts (stringing, blobs)
  2. 320–400 grit: Smooth out the scratches from the coarse sandpaper
  3. 600–800 grit: Final smoothing before primer (optional for non-cosplay pieces)

Key rules:

  • Sand in one direction per grit — don't scrub back and forth
  • Use a sanding block on flat surfaces (fingers create uneven pressure)
  • Wet-sand above 320 grit to prevent clogging
  • Don't skip grits — each step removes the scratches from the previous one

For Intricate Details

Use needle files, sanding sticks, or a Dremel with soft buffs for details that fingers can't reach. A Dremel speeds things up dramatically but can melt PLA if you're not careful — use low speed and light pressure.

Step 2: Priming — The Critical Layer

Primer does three things: fills micro-scratches, creates a uniform surface for paint adhesion, and reveals remaining imperfections you missed while sanding.

Choosing a Primer

  • Sandable primer (auto primer): The best option. Fills layer lines, can be sanded between coats for a glass-smooth finish.
  • Plastic adhesion primer: Designed specifically for plastics. Best for PETG and ABS.
  • Filler primer: Thicker than regular primer. Great for larger layer lines or rough surfaces.

Application Technique

  1. Shake the can for 2 minutes
  2. Hold 12–15 inches from the surface
  3. Apply 2–3 thin coats, waiting 10–15 minutes between each
  4. After the final coat, let dry for 24 hours
  5. Lightly sand with 400-grit
  6. Apply one more thin primer coat for a final smooth base

Common mistake: Applying one heavy coat instead of multiple thin ones. Heavy coats drip, run, and create texture you'll have to sand off anyway.

Step 3: Painting — Bringing It to Life

Acrylic Paint

The most versatile option. Water-based, easy to clean up, and available in thousands of colors. Acrylics work well for detail work, layering, and blending.

Best for: Detail work, small pieces, gifts, display models

Spray Paint

Fast, even coverage for larger pieces. Rust-Oleum and Krylon both make plastic-compatible spray paints. Apply in thin coats the same way as primer.

Best for: Large flat surfaces, armor pieces, base coats

Enamel Paint

Harder, more durable finish than acrylic. Takes longer to dry but resists chipping better. Often used for model kits and miniatures.

Best for: High-wear items, outdoor pieces, pieces that will be handled frequently

Painting by Filament Type

Filament Paint Adhesion Special Considerations
PLA Good with primer Avoid heat-based techniques (PLA warps)
PETG Fair (needs adhesion primer) Lightly scuff-sand before priming
ABS Good with primer Can be acetone-smoothed before painting

Step 4: Sealing — Protecting Your Work

Paint without a clear coat will chip, scratch, and fade. A sealant protects everything underneath.

Clear Coat Options

  • Matte clear coat: Natural, non-shiny finish. Best for props that shouldn't look "plastic"
  • Satin clear coat: Subtle sheen. Good middle ground
  • Gloss clear coat: High shine. Best for automotive finishes, helmets, and anything meant to look polished

Apply 2–3 thin coats, same technique as primer. Let each coat dry fully before the next.

Advanced Techniques

Dry Brushing

Load a brush with paint, wipe most of it off on a paper towel, then lightly brush over raised details. The remaining paint catches only the high points, creating highlights and a weathered look. Essential for cosplay props.

Wash / Patina

Apply a thin, dark wash (watered-down paint) over the surface. The wash flows into crevices and recesses, creating shadows and depth. Wipe off the excess from raised areas with a damp cloth before it dries.

Chipping Effect

Paint a base color (the "underneath" color — rust, bare metal), apply chipping medium, then paint your top coat. Rub the top coat with water and a stiff brush to reveal the base color underneath. This creates realistic wear patterns.

Two-Tone Painting

Mask areas with painter's tape before painting the first color. Remove the tape, then mask the painted areas and apply the second color. Crisp lines require crisp masking — press tape edges firmly.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Skip sanding: Paint won't hide layer lines — it accentuates them
  • Heavy primer coat: Creates drips and texture. Multiple thin coats always win
  • No clear coat: Your paint job will chip within days of handling
  • Painting in high humidity: Causes "blushing" (white, cloudy patches in clear coat)
  • Impatience: Let each layer dry fully. Rushed work shows

Conclusion

Painting transforms 3D prints from obviously-printed objects into professional-looking pieces. The process is straightforward: sand smooth, prime properly, paint with care, and seal for protection. Each step builds on the previous one, and skipping any of them shows in the final result.

Start with a simple piece — a phone stand or a keychain — and practice the full workflow before tackling a complex project. Your first painted piece won't be perfect, but your fifth will be dramatically better. That's the progression.
