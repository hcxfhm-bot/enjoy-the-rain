PETG vs PLA: Which Filament Should You Actually Use?

📅 May 5, 2026 ⏱️ 9 min read ✍️ 3D Print Starter Team
⚖️

The Short Answer First

PLA is easier and biodegradable. PETG is stronger and more heat-resistant but trickier to print. Neither is "better" — they serve different purposes. If you only want to buy one spool right now and you're just getting started, go with PLA. It's forgiving, widely available, and you can start printing almost immediately without fighting your settings.

But if you want parts that actually survive real-world use — things that need to handle heat, stress, or outdoor conditions — PETG is where you need to go. Just know it demands more attention. Your nozzle temperature, print speed, and cooling fan all matter in ways PLA simply doesn't. If you're printing on something like an Ender 3 V3 SE, you'll notice the difference immediately when switching between the two. And if you're looking for the right temperature windows for your PLA, check out our PLA temperature guide — getting those numbers right will save you a lot of failed prints.

What Is PLA?

PLA stands for polylactic acid. It's made from corn starch or sugarcane, which sounds almost too friendly for a plastic. Under the right industrial composting conditions, it actually biodegrades — though let's be real, that takes years and specific heat and moisture levels. In your average landfill, it sits around just like any other plastic.

When you print it, you'll notice a faintly sweet smell — nothing like the acrid, nose-burning fumes of ABS. That alone makes a difference if you're printing in a bedroom or small space. It's the most popular filament on the market for a reason. Beginners love it because it prints at relatively low temperatures (190–220°C), rarely warps, and sticks to the bed like it wants to be there.

Where PLA falls short: it's brittle. Drop a PLA print on a hard floor and there's a good chance it cracks or shatters. It softens at around 55°C, so leaving it in a hot car is a bad idea. And UV light will degrade it over time. Great for desktop trinkets, cosplay helmets that won't get hammered, and prototypes that won't be load-bearing.

What Is PETG?

PETG is polyethylene terephthalate glycol. Same plastic used in water bottles, but modified with glycol to make it play nicer for 3D printing. The "G" is the key — it improves layer adhesion and makes the material less brittle than regular PET.

One of PETG's standout features is how good it looks in transparent or clear prints. If you want to print something that lets light through, PETG is your best bet among accessible filaments. It's also significantly more UV-resistant than PLA, meaning it holds up outdoors without degrading the way PLA does.

Food safety is a gray area worth addressing. Pure PETG can be food-safe when printed properly — meaning a food-grade filament, a nozzle without heavy metals (skip brass if you're using glass-filled PETG), and a printer that hasn't been contaminated with other materials. But for most home printers, "food safe" is aspirational at best. Don't bet your health on it without serious due diligence.

Head-to-Head Comparison

Here's where it gets practical. Let's break it down category by category:

  • Strength: PETG wins, full stop. It bends before it breaks. PLA snaps. If your print needs to absorb any kind of impact, PETG is the answer.
  • Ease of printing: PLA wins. Less stringing, better layer adhesion, minimal warping. You can print PLA on almost any machine out of the box.
  • Heat resistance: PETG wins. PETG softens around 80°C. PLA starts getting soft at 55°C. If your print will see any warmth — a dashboard, a sunny window — this matters a lot.
  • UV resistance: PETG wins. PLA degrades in sunlight. PETG handles it. Outdoor use? PETG every time.
  • Print speed: PLA wins. You can push PLA faster without sacrificing quality. PETG needs to be printed slower to get good layer bonding.
  • Cost: Roughly equal. Both typically run $20–25 per kilogram spool. You won't save money choosing one over the other.
  • Food safety: PETG has more potential but needs specific nozzles (hardened steel for glass-filled varieties). PLA has similar considerations. Neither is automatically food-safe in a home setup.
  • Biodegradability: PLA wins on paper. In practice, both last for years in normal disposal conditions, so don't let this drive your decision too much.

When to Use PLA

PLA is your go-to for decorative items, figurines, rapid prototypes, cosplay props, and school projects. Anything that lives indoors, won't be exposed to heat, and doesn't need to survive being dropped is a great PLA candidate.

If you're after easy, setup-and-forget printing — where you can walk away and trust the print will finish — PLA is far more forgiving.堵

It also wins if you're printing in a space without great ventilation. PLA's fumes are milder. That's not a license to run it in a sealed closet, but it's noticeably less aggressive than ABS and more tolerable than PETG for extended sessions.

Forgetting your temperature settings is the most common beginner mistake. Our PLA temperature guide walks through exactly what to set on popular machines and why those numbers matter.

When to Use PETG

PETG is the material for functional parts. If your print needs to do a job — bear weight, handle friction, sit outside, or survive being dropped — PETG is the right call. Drone parts, custom tools, brackets, mounts, and anything that goes under the hood of a car (or in direct sunlight) belongs in PETG.

One of PETG's underrated strengths is that it flexes slightly under load rather than cracking. That elasticity makes it far more suitable for parts that need some give. It also holds screws and bolts better than PLA, which can strip or crack under fastener stress.

On this site, PETG is our default recommendation for functional prints. If you're building something that matters, something that needs to actually work — PETG is almost always the right starting point. Machines like the Bambu Lab A1 Mini handle PETG beautifully out of the box, making it easier than ever to get solid functional parts on a modest budget.

Can You Print PETG on a PLA Profile?

No. If you try to run PETG with the same settings you'd use for PLA, you'll get frustration and likely a tangled mess of filament — commonly called "spaghetti mode" in the community.

PETG demands real adjustments. Your nozzle needs to be 230–250°C, not the 200°C that works fine for PLA. Your bed should sit at 70–90°C. And your cooling fan? Run it at 30–50% — PETG needs a bit of warmth to bond layers properly. Blast it with full fan like you would PLA and your layers won't fuse.

Print speed also needs to drop. Target 50–60mm/s for PETG. Going faster causes stringing and delamination. Slower prints are stronger prints with this material.

Skipping these adjustments is the number one reason people fail with PETG and then decide it's "hard." It's not hard — it's just different. Respect the material and it will deliver.

Bottom Line

New to 3D printing? Start with PLA. Learn the basics of your machine, understand how slicing works, and get comfortable before adding complexity.

Ready to make parts that actually function? Learn PETG. The extra settings effort pays off in durability, heat resistance, and outdoor viability.

Keep both on hand. Most printers do. PLA handles your models, figurines, and quick prototypes. PETG handles everything that needs to survive real conditions. Think of them as complementary tools, not competitors. Your first spool should be PLA. Your second should probably be PETG — and once you've dialed in your settings, you'll find yourself reaching for it more than you expected.
