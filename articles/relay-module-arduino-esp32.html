⚠️ Safety Warning

Relay modules are often used to control 120V/240V mains electricity. Incorrect wiring can cause fire, electric shock, or death. If you're not comfortable working with high voltage, use low-voltage DC devices only (12V LED strips, 5V fans, etc.) for learning. For mains-voltage projects, consult a licensed electrician.

Why You Need a Relay: Bridging Low-Voltage Electronics and High-Voltage Appliances

Here's the problem every maker eventually hits: Your Arduino or ESP32 can only output 3.3V or 5V at 20–40 milliamps. That's enough to blink an LED, but it won't power a lamp, a coffee maker, a water pump, or a garage door opener. Trying to connect a 120V appliance directly to an Arduino pin will instantly destroy the microcontroller—and possibly start a fire.

This is where relays come in. A relay is an electrically operated switch. It uses a small low-voltage signal from your Arduino to control a separate high-voltage/high-current circuit. Think of it as a "remote-controlled wall switch" that your microcontroller can click on and off.

In the smart home world, relays are the backbone of automation. They let you:

  • Turn lights on/off via a web dashboard
  • Build a garage door opener that works from your phone
  • Automatically water your garden based on soil moisture sensor data (see our Electronics Sensor Guide for sensor basics)
  • Control a space heater with a ESP32 running ESPHome and Home Assistant
  • Build a 3D printer enclosure with active heating and exhaust fan control

This guide covers everything you need to safely and confidently use relay modules with Arduino and ESP32. By the end, you'll be able to wire, code, and deploy relay-based projects that actually work reliably—not just on your desk, but in your home.

Relay Basics: NO, NC, COM, and How a Relay Actually Works

Before touching wires, you need to understand what's happening inside a relay. skip this, and you'll inevitably wire something wrong.

What's Inside a Relay?

A relay has two completely isolated sides:

  1. Low-voltage side (control side): Connected to your Arduino/ESP32. When you send a signal (HIGH), it energizes a coil inside the relay, creating a magnetic field.
  2. High-voltage side (switch side): Connected to the device you want to control (lamp, pump, etc.). The magnetic field from the coil physically pulls a metal arm that closes (or opens) the high-voltage circuit.

Key point: The two sides are electrically isolated. Even if the high-voltage side is carrying 240V AC, it won't feed back into your Arduino. That's the entire point of a relay.

Understanding NO, NC, and COM Terminals

Relay modules have screw terminals labeled NO, NC, and COM. Here's what they mean:

  • COM (Common): The "middle" terminal. Connect your power source (the "hot" wire from your power supply or mains) to COM.
  • NO (Normally Open): When the relay is OFF (no signal), this terminal is not connected to COM. When you send a signal to activate the relay, the switch closes and COM connects to NO. Use NO if you want the device to be OFF by default and turn ON when triggered.
  • NC (Normally Closed): The opposite. When the relay is OFF, COM is connected to NC. When you activate the relay, the connection breaks. Use NC if you want the device to be ON by default and turn OFF when triggered.
Terminal Relay OFF Relay ON Typical Use Case
NO (Normally Open) Open (no connection) Closed (connected to COM) Lights, fans, pumps (device OFF by default)
NC (Normally Closed) Closed (connected to COM) Open (disconnected) Security lights, alarms (device ON by default)
COM (Common) Connect to power source (hot wire) Always used with NO or NC

Beginner mistake #1: Wiring your device to NC and wondering why it turns on as soon as you plug in the Arduino. Double-check NO vs NC before powering anything.

Active HIGH vs Active LOW Relay Modules

Most relay modules can be configured as "Active HIGH" or "Active LOW" by changing a jumper (or soldering a trace):

  • Active HIGH: Sending HIGH (5V or 3.3V) to the IN pin activates the relay. This is the most intuitive and what most Arduino code examples assume.
  • Active LOW: Sending LOW (0V/GND) activates the relay. Some ESP32/ESP8266 projects use this because it simplifies wiring when using certain pins.

Check your module's jumper! Many cheap modules ship in Active LOW mode, which confuses beginners who expect HIGH to turn the relay on.

Relay Module Types: 1-Channel, 2-Channel, 4-Channel, and 8-Channel Compared

Relay modules come in different channel counts. Which one you need depends on how many devices you want to control simultaneously.

1-Channel Relay Module (SRD-05VDC-SL-C)

  • Price: $1–2
  • Best for: Single-device projects (one lamp, one fan, one pump)
  • Pins: VCC, GND, IN1
  • Terminals: NO, NC, COM

This is the "hello world" of relay modules. If you're learning, start here. The SRD-05VDC-SL-C is the most common 1-channel module on AliExpress and Amazon. It has a nice blue relay with a clear "click" when it activates.

2-Channel Relay Module

  • Price: $3–5
  • Best for: Projects needing two switches (e.g., exhaust fan + heater, or two separate lights)
  • Pins: VCC, GND, IN1, IN2
  • Terminals: Two sets of NO/NC/COM

4-Channel and 8-Channel Relay Modules

  • Price: $8–15 (4-channel), $15–25 (8-channel)
  • Best for: Smart home panels controlling multiple lights/appliances from a single ESP32
  • Pins: VCC, GND, IN1–IN4 (or IN8)
  • Power note: 8-channel modules often need a separate 5V power supply (they draw ~400mA when all relays are on). Don't try to power one from an Arduino's 5V pin.
Module Price Current Draw (all on) Use Case
1-Channel $1–2 ~70mA Learning, single device
2-Channel $3–5 ~140mA Fan + heater, 2 lights
4-Channel $8–12 ~280mA Smart home light panel
8-Channel $15–25 ~560mA Whole-room automation

Wiring a Relay Module to Arduino (5V Modules)

Most relay modules are designed for 5V logic (they expect a 5V signal to activate). This makes them perfect for Arduino Uno, Nano, and Mega, which all have 5V GPIO pins.

Wiring Diagram (1-Channel Module + Arduino Uno)

Arduino Uno         1-Channel Relay Module
-----------         ------------------------
5V          →       VCC
GND         →       GND
Pin 7       →       IN1

Relay Terminals:
COM         →       5V from external power supply (for low-voltage DC projects)
                  OR hot wire from mains (for AC projects—see safety section!)
NO          →       Positive wire of the device you're controlling
Device GND  →       GND of external power supply (complete the circuit)

Important: The relay module's VCC and GND power the relay coil (the low-voltage side). The high-voltage side (COM/NO/NC) is completely separate—you can use a 5V relay module to switch 12V DC, 24V DC, or even 240V AC. The relay doesn't care about the voltage on the switch side, as long as it's within its rating (usually 10A at 250V AC).

Arduino Code (Active HIGH)

// Simple relay test: click relay on/off every 2 seconds

#define RELAY_PIN 7

void setup() {
  pinMode(RELAY_PIN, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(RELAY_PIN, LOW); // Start with relay OFF
  Serial.begin(9600);
}

void loop() {
  Serial.println("Relay ON");
  digitalWrite(RELAY_PIN, HIGH);  // Activate relay
  delay(2000);

  Serial.println("Relay OFF");
  digitalWrite(RELAY_PIN, LOW);   // Deactivate relay
  delay(2000);
}

Troubleshooting tip: If the relay clicks but your device doesn't turn on, check that you've wired the high-voltage side correctly. The relay clicking means the coil is working—the problem is in your COM/NO wiring.

Wiring a Relay Module to ESP32 (3.3V Logic—Critical Differences!)

Here's where many makers run into trouble: ESP32 GPIO pins output 3.3V, but most relay modules expect 5V on the IN pin to activate. If you connect an ESP32 directly to a standard 5V relay module, the relay might not activate reliably (or at all).

Solution 1: Use a 3.3V-Compatible Relay Module

Some relay modules (often labeled "3.3V/5V compatible") have a voltage comparator circuit that accepts both 3.3V and 5V signals. These are the easiest option for ESP32 projects. Look for modules with an "SRD-03VDC-SL-C" relay or modules that explicitly say "ESP32 compatible" in the listing.

Solution 2: Use a Logic Level Converter or Transistor

If you only have a 5V relay module, you can use a 2N2222 NPN transistor or a logic level converter to step up the ESP32's 3.3V signal to 5V. Here's the wiring:

ESP32 GPIO 18 ──→ 1kΩ resistor ──→ Base of 2N2222
2N2222 Emitter ──→ GND
2N2222 Collector ──→ Relay IN pin
Relay VCC ──→ External 5V power supply
Relay GND ──→ GND (shared with ESP32 GND)

This works because the transistor acts as a low-side switch: when the ESP32 sends 3.3V to the base, the transistor allows current to flow from the 5V supply through the relay coil to GND.

Solution 3: Use an ESP32 Board with 5V-Compatible Pins

Some ESP32 development boards (like the ESP32-WROVER-KIT) have 5V-tolerant pins when configured as outputs with pull-up resistors disabled. However, this is not officially recommended by Espressif. Use Solution 1 or 2 instead.

ESP32 Sample Code (using ESPHome or Arduino framework)

// ESP32 + Relay (using Arduino framework)
// Use GPIO 18 (safe for output, not an input-only pin)

#define RELAY_PIN 18

void setup() {
  pinMode(RELAY_PIN, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(RELAY_PIN, LOW);
  Serial.begin(115200);
}

void loop() {
  // Toggle relay every 3 seconds
  digitalWrite(RELAY_PIN, HIGH);
  Serial.println("ON");
  delay(3000);

  digitalWrite(RELAY_PIN, LOW);
  Serial.println("OFF");
  delay(3000);
}

Why this matters for smart home projects: If you're building a DIY smart door lock or a Home Assistant automation, you'll likely use an ESP32 (it has Wi-Fi built in). Getting the relay wiring right is critical for reliability.

Sample Code: Button-Triggered Relay, Delay Timers, and Web Control

Let's look at three practical code examples that go beyond "blinking" the relay.

Example 1: Button-Triggered Relay (with Debouncing)

// Press a button to toggle the relay state
// Includes software debouncing to prevent flickering

#define RELAY_PIN 7
#define BUTTON_PIN 2

bool relayState = false;
bool lastButtonState = HIGH;
unsigned long lastDebounceTime = 0;
const unsigned long debounceDelay = 50;

void setup() {
  pinMode(RELAY_PIN, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(BUTTON_PIN, INPUT_PULLUP); // Internal pull-up resistor
  digitalWrite(RELAY_PIN, LOW);
  Serial.begin(9600);
}

void loop() {
  bool reading = digitalRead(BUTTON_PIN);

  if (reading != lastButtonState) {
    lastDebounceTime = millis();
  }

  if ((millis() - lastDebounceTime) > debounceDelay) {
    if (reading == LOW) {  // Button pressed (INPUT_PULLUP = LOW when pressed)
      relayState = !relayState;
      digitalWrite(RELAY_PIN, relayState ? HIGH : LOW);
      Serial.print("Relay: ");
      Serial.println(relayState ? "ON" : "OFF");
      delay(200); // Simple way to prevent multiple triggers
    }
  }

  lastButtonState = reading;
}

Example 2: Relay with Delay Timer (Turn Off After 10 Minutes)

// Turn on a device for exactly 10 minutes, then auto-off
// Useful for exhaust fans, grow lights, etc.

#define RELAY_PIN 7

const unsigned long ON_DURATION = 10UL * 60UL * 1000UL; // 10 minutes in ms
unsigned long turnOnTime = 0;
bool timerActive = false;

void setup() {
  pinMode(RELAY_PIN, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(RELAY_PIN, LOW);
  Serial.begin(9600);
  Serial.println("Send '1' to turn on for 10 minutes");
}

void loop() {
  if (Serial.available()) {
    char c = Serial.read();
    if (c == '1' && !timerActive) {
      digitalWrite(RELAY_PIN, HIGH);
      turnOnTime = millis();
      timerActive = true;
      Serial.println("Relay ON — will turn off in 10 minutes");
    }
  }

  if (timerActive && (millis() - turnOnTime >= ON_DURATION)) {
    digitalWrite(RELAY_PIN, LOW);
    timerActive = false;
    Serial.println("Relay OFF (timer expired)");
  }
}

Example 3: ESP32 Web Server to Control Relay (No Home Assistant Needed!)

For a simple project, you can control the relay from a web page hosted directly on the ESP32. This is perfect for a "smart switch" that doesn't need Home Assistant.

// ESP32 web server relay control (Arduino framework + WiFi)
// Access via browser at http://[ESP32-IP]/on or /off

#include <WiFi.h>
#include <WebServer.h>

const char* ssid = "YOUR_WIFI_SSID";
const char* password = "YOUR_WIFI_PASSWORD";
#define RELAY_PIN 18

WebServer server(80);

void handleRoot() {
  String html = "<html><body>";
  html += "<h1>Relay Control</h1>";
  html += "<a href='/on'><button>Turn ON</button></a>";
  html += "<a href='/off'><button>Turn OFF</button></a>";
  html += "</body></html>";
  server.send(200, "text/html", html);
}

void handleOn() {
  digitalWrite(RELAY_PIN, HIGH);
  server.send(200, "text/html", "<a href='/'>Back</a> — Relay is ON");
}

void handleOff() {
  digitalWrite(RELAY_PIN, LOW);
  server.send(200, "text/html", "<a href='/'>Back</a> — Relay is OFF");
}

void setup() {
  pinMode(RELAY_PIN, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(RELAY_PIN, LOW);
  WiFi.begin(ssid, password);
  while (WiFi.status() != WL_CONNECTED) delay(500);
  
  server.on("/", handleRoot);
  server.on("/on", handleOn);
  server.on("/off", handleOff);
  server.begin();
}

void loop() {
  server.handleClient();
}

Pro tip: For serious home automation, skip the custom web server and flash your ESP32 with ESPHome. It integrates directly with Home Assistant and gives you a proper UI, automation rules, and OTA updates.

Safety First: High-Voltage Warnings and Best Practices

I can't emphasize this enough: relays are often used to switch mains voltage (120V AC in the US, 230V AC in Europe/China). This can kill you. Follow these rules strictly.

Rule 1: Never Work on Live Mains Circuits

Always unplug the device before touching any wires. Use a non-contact voltage tester to confirm the circuit is dead. If you don't know which wire is "hot," don't guess—use a multimeter.

Rule 2: Use Proper Enclosures

Don't leave a relay module sitting on your desk with exposed mains wires. Use a 3D-printed enclosure (see our guide to 3D printing smart home cases) or a proper DIN rail enclosure. The enclosure should be non-conductive and have strain relief for cables.

Rule 3: Respect the Relay's Current Rating

Most relay modules are rated for 10A at 250V AC. That's about 2500 watts. Don't exceed this. If you're controlling a heater that draws 2000W, you're fine. If you're controlling an electric oven that draws 4000W, you need a contactor (a heavy-duty relay), not a tiny SRD-05VDC-SL-C.

Rule 4: Use Snubber Circuits for Inductive Loads

Inductive loads (motors, solenoids, fluorescent lights) create voltage spikes when switched off. These spikes can damage the relay contacts or cause electromagnetic interference. Use an RC snubber circuit across the load, or choose a relay with built-in arc suppression.

Rule 5: Keep Low-Voltage and High-Voltage Wiring Separate

Route the Arduino/ESP32 wires (the 3.3V/5V control wires) away from the mains wires. Use cable glands and separate compartments in your enclosure. This isn't just for safety—it also reduces electromagnetic interference that can cause your microcontroller to reset.

💡 Beginner Project Idea: Start with 12V DC

If you're new to relays, don't start with mains voltage. Use a 12V LED strip or a 12V fan as your "high-voltage" load. Wire the relay to switch the 12V supply. Once you've got that working reliably, then consider moving to mains—and even then, consider hiring an electrician to do the final wiring.

5 Practical Smart Home Projects Using Relays

Now that you know how to wire and code a relay, here are five projects to try. Each one teaches a different skill.

Project 1: Smart Garage Door Opener (ESP32 + Relay + Magnetic Sensor)

Use a relay to "press" the garage door button (the relay contacts act as a remote push button). Add a magnetic reed switch to detect if the door is open or closed. Flash the ESP32 with ESPHome and integrate with Home Assistant for phone-based control.

Skills learned: Momentary switch simulation, sensor integration, ESPHome configuration.

Project 2: Automatic Grow Light (Arduino + Relay + DHT22 Sensor)

Connect a DHT22 temperature/humidity sensor to an Arduino. Program it to turn on a grow light (via relay) when the temperature drops below a threshold, or on a daily schedule using a real-time clock (RTC) module.

Skills learned: Sensor integration, scheduling, environmental control.

Project 3: Smart Power Strip (ESP32 + 4-Channel Relay Module)

Build a power strip where each outlet is controlled independently via Wi-Fi. Use a 4-channel relay module and an ESP32. Print a custom enclosure (see our 3D printing guide for smart home cases). Add current sensors (ACS712) to monitor power usage per outlet.

Skills learned: Multi-channel relay control, power monitoring, enclosure design.

Project 4: Sump Pump Auto-Switch (Arduino + Relay + Water Level Sensor)

Use a water level sensor (or float switch) to detect when a sump pit is full. Activate a relay that turns on a water pump. Include a manual override button and a buzzer alarm for when the pump fails to start.

Skills learned: Fail-safe design, alarm integration, pump control.

Project 5: 3D Printer Enclosure Exhaust Fan (ESP32 + Relay + Temperature Sensor)

If you print ABS, you need an enclosure with active ventilation. Use an ESP32 to read the enclosure temperature (DS18B20 sensor) and turn on an exhaust fan via relay when the temperature exceeds 50°C. Integrate with Home Assistant to get notifications when the fan turns on.

Skills learned: Thermal management, notifications, 3D printer accessories.

Common Relay Problems and How to Fix Them

Problem 1: Relay Clicks, But Device Doesn't Turn On

Cause: Incorrect wiring on the high-voltage side (COM/NO/NC). The relay coil is working (you hear the click), but the switch side isn't making contact with your device.

Fix: Use a multimeter in continuity mode. With the relay OFF, check if there's continuity between COM and NC (there should be). Activate the relay and check if there's continuity between COM and NO (there should be now). If not, your module is defective.

Problem 2: ESP32 Won't Activate the Relay (3.3V Logic Issue)

Cause: Your relay module expects 5V on the IN pin, but the ESP32 only outputs 3.3V.

Fix: Use a 3.3V-compatible relay module, or add a transistor/level shifter (see the ESP32 wiring section above).

Problem 3: Relay Triggers Randomly or Resets the Microcontroller

Cause: Voltage spike when the relay coil de-energizes (back-EMF), or insufficient power supply current.

Fix: Add a flyback diode across the relay coil (most modules already have one, but cheap ones might not). Use a separate power supply for the relay module (don't power it from the Arduino's 5V pin if you're switching multiple relays).

Problem 4: Relay Sticks (Won't Turn Off Even When Signal Is Removed)

Cause: Relay contacts have welded together due to overcurrent or arcing.

Fix: Replace the relay module. For the future, use a relay rated for at least 2× your expected load current. If you're switching a motor, add a snubber circuit to reduce arcing.

Problem 5: Device Turns On Briefly, Then Turns Off Immediately

Cause: Insufficient power supply. When the relay activates, it draws a surge of current that causes the microcontroller to brown out and reset.

Fix: Use a power supply that can deliver at least 500mA for a single relay module, more for multi-channel. Add a capacitor (100µF–1000µF) across the relay module's VCC and GND pins to smooth out power surges.

Relay Module Buying Guide: What to Look For

Not all relay modules are created equal. Here's what to check before buying:

1. Voltage Compatibility

  • Arduino projects: 5V relay modules (SRD-05VDC-SL-C)
  • ESP32/ESP8266 projects: 3.3V-compatible modules (SRD-03VDC-SL-C) or use a level shifter
  • Raspberry Pi projects: 3.3V or 5V (Pi GPIO is 3.3V, but you can use a 5V module with a transistor)

2. Current Rating

The relay's contact rating (usually 10A at 250V AC) is for resistive loads (heaters, lights). For inductive loads (motors, pumps), derate by 50%. A 10A relay is only good for about 5A of motor load.

3. Optocoupler Isolation

Quality relay modules include an optocoupler (like the PC817) on the input side. This provides electrical isolation between your microcontroller and the relay coil, reducing noise and preventing damage from voltage spikes. Cheap modules without optocouplers are more likely to cause random resets or damaged GPIO pins.

4. Status LEDs

A good relay module has an LED that lights up when the relay is activated. This seems trivial, but it's a huge help for debugging. If the LED is on but the relay isn't clicking, you know the problem is on the coil side. If the LED is off, the problem is in your code or wiring to the IN pin.

5. Terminal Type

Some modules use screw terminals (easy to wire, secure), others use spring terminals (faster to wire, but harder to insert thick wires). For mains voltage, screw terminals are preferred—they provide a more reliable connection that won't vibrate loose over time.

Feature Budget Module ($1–2) Quality Module ($3–5)
Optocoupler isolation ❌ Often missing ✅ Included
Status LED ✅ Usually present ✅ Present
Active HIGH/LOW jumper ❌ Fixed mode ✅ Adjustable
Terminal type Screw terminal Screw terminal (better quality)
Relay brand Generic (Songle, etc.) Name brand (Omron, Songle with UL certification)
