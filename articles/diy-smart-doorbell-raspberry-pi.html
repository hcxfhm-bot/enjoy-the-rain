How to Build a DIY Smart Doorbell with Raspberry Pi and Home Assistant

📅 July 30, 2026 ⏱️ 14 min read ✍️ 3D Print Starter Team
🔔

Commercial smart doorbells like Ring, Nest, and Eufy cost $100–$250 — and they often lock you into monthly cloud subscription fees just to view recorded footage. But here's the secret: you can build a DIY smart doorbell for under $50 that's entirely local, has no subscription costs, and integrates natively with Home Assistant.

In this guide, I'll walk you through building a complete Raspberry Pi doorbell system: a Raspberry Pi running Home Assistant as your brain, an ESP32-CAM module for live video, a physical doorbell button that triggers push notifications to your phone, and a 3D printed enclosure for a polished look. No cloud dependency, no monthly fees — just pure, private smart home goodness.

This is one of the most rewarding Raspberry Pi DIY projects you can build, and by the end of this tutorial, you'll have a fully functional video doorbell that rivals anything on the market.

How This DIY Smart Doorbell Works

Before we dive into the build, here's a quick overview of the system architecture:

  • Raspberry Pi — Runs Home Assistant OS, the central brain that connects everything together
  • ESP32-CAM — Streams live video from your front door and detects motion events
  • Momentary push button — A physical doorbell button connected to the ESP32
  • Home Assistant automations — Send push notifications with a snapshot to your phone when someone rings the bell
  • 3D printed enclosure — A weatherproof housing for the ESP32-CAM and button

When a visitor presses the button, the ESP32 sends an MQTT message to Home Assistant, which immediately triggers a notification on your phone with a live camera snapshot. You can then view the video feed, talk through your phone, or trigger automations like unlocking a smart door lock — all from the same dashboard.

Step 1: Set Up Home Assistant on Your Raspberry Pi

If you haven't already installed Home Assistant, now's the time. We have a complete Home Assistant on Raspberry Pi setup guide, but here's the quick version:

  1. Download Home Assistant OS — Grab the Raspberry Pi-specific image from the official Home Assistant website
  2. Flash the image — Use Raspberry Pi Imager or Balena Etcher to flash the OS to your microSD card
  3. Boot and configure — Insert the card into your Pi, connect Ethernet (or configure Wi-Fi on the card beforehand), and power it on
  4. Access the web UI — After a few minutes, visit http://homeassistant.local:8123 to complete the setup wizard
  5. Install the ESPHome add-on — From the Supervisor panel, search for ESPHome and install it

Once Home Assistant is running, you'll also want to install the MQTT broker add-on (Mosquitto). The ESP32-CAM communicates via MQTT, and having a local broker keeps everything fast and private.

Step 2: Flash ESPHome Firmware to the ESP32-CAM

The ESP32-CAM comes with no firmware — you have to program it yourself. We'll use ESPHome, which integrates natively with Home Assistant and requires almost no coding.

2.1 Wire the FTDI Programmer to the ESP32-CAM

For the first flash, you need an FTDI programmer connected like this:

FTDI Pin ESP32-CAM Pin
3.3V (VCC) 3.3V
GND GND
TX U0R (RX)
RX U0T (TX)
DTR → RTS GPIO0 → GND (for flash mode)

Important: To enter flash mode, connect GPIO0 to GND before powering on the ESP32-CAM. This tells the chip to listen for new firmware. Disconnect GPIO0 from GND after flashing.

2.2 Create the ESPHome Device

In the ESPHome add-on dashboard, click "+ New Device" and paste this configuration:

esphome:
  name: front-door-doorbell
  platform: ESP32
  board: esp32dev  # AI-Thinker ESP32-CAM variant

wifi:
  ssid: "Your_WiFi_Name"
  password: "Your_WiFi_Password"

# Enable fallback hotspot in case WiFi fails
captive_portal:

# Enable logging
logger:

# Enable Home Assistant API
api:

# OTA updates
ota:

mqtt:
  broker: 192.168.1.100  # Your Home Assistant IP
  topic_prefix: doorbell/front

esp32_camera:
  external_clock:
    pin: GPIO0
    frequency: 20MHz
  i2c_pins:
    sda: GPIO26
    scl: GPIO27
  data_pins: [GPIO5, GPIO18, GPIO19, GPIO21, GPIO36, GPIO39, GPIO34, GPIO35]
  vsync_pin: GPIO25
  href_pin: GPIO23
  pixel_clock_pin: GPIO22
  power_down_pin: GPIO32
  resolution: 800x600

# Doorbell button on GPIO13
binary_sensor:
  - platform: gpio
    pin:
      number: GPIO13
      mode: INPUT_PULLUP
    name: "Front Doorbell Button"
    device_class: occupancy
    filters:
      - delayed_on: 10ms
      - delayed_off: 10ms
    on_press:
      - mqtt.publish:
          topic: doorbell/front/pressed
          payload: "ON"

# Keep the camera powered for streaming
switch:
  - platform: gpio
    pin: GPIO4
    name: "Camera Flash"
    id: flash_light
    icon: "mdi:flashlight"

This configuration sets up the ESP32-CAM to stream video and monitor a doorbell button on GPIO13. When the button is pressed, it publishes an MQTT message that Home Assistant can trigger on.

2.3 Flash and Test

Plug the FTDI programmer into your computer, click "Install" in ESPHome, and choose "Plug into this computer." After the flash completes (about 60 seconds), unplug GPIO0 from GND and power-cycle the ESP32-CAM. You should see a live camera stream in the ESPHome logs.

Step 3: Configure Home Assistant Automations

Now for the fun part — making your Home Assistant doorbell actually do something useful. We'll create an automation that sends a push notification with a snapshot when the button is pressed.

3.1 Install the Camera Entity

Once the ESP32-CAM connects to your network, ESPHome automatically creates a camera entity in Home Assistant. Go to Settings → Devices & Services → ESPHome and you should see "front-door-doorbell" with a camera entity ready to use.

3.2 Create the Automation

Navigate to Settings → Automations & Scenes and create a new automation. Paste this YAML:

alias: "Front Doorbell Pressed"
description: "Notify when someone rings the doorbell"
trigger:
  - platform: mqtt
    topic: doorbell/front/pressed
    payload: "ON"
action:
  - service: camera.snapshot
    target:
      entity_id: camera.front_door_doorbell
    data:
      filename: /media/snapshots/doorbell_{{ now().strftime('%Y%m%d_%H%M%S') }}.jpg
  - delay:
      milliseconds: 500
  - service: notify.mobile_app_your_phone
    data:
      title: "🔔 Doorbell Ring!"
      message: "Someone is at the front door"
      data:
        image: /media/snapshots/doorbell_{{ now().strftime('%Y%m%d_%H%M%S') }}.jpg
        url: /lovelace/doorbell
mode: single

Replace notify.mobile_app_your_phone with your actual mobile app notification service (it appears automatically after you install the Home Assistant Companion app on your phone).

Step 4: 3D Print a Weatherproof Enclosure

This is where your 3D printer shines. Instead of zip-tying a bare circuit board to your doorframe, you can print a professional-looking enclosure that's weather-resistant and integrates the camera lens and button perfectly.

Design Considerations for an Outdoor Enclosure

  • Material choice — Use PETG or ASA filament. PLA will warp in direct sunlight and degrade from UV exposure. PETG offers a great balance of printability and outdoor durability.
  • Camera lens window — Leave a precisely sized hole for the camera lens. You can press-fit a piece of acrylic or clear resin behind the opening for weather protection.
  • Button seal — Use a silicone gasket or rubber O-ring around the push button to prevent water ingress.
  • Ventilation — Small drainage holes at the bottom prevent moisture buildup inside the enclosure.
  • Mounting — Design screw holes or a slide-on bracket so you can mount it securely without adhesives.

You can find ready-made ESP32-CAM doorbell enclosure STL files on Printables and Thingiverse, or design your own. If you're new to enclosure design, our guide on designing and 3D printing custom ESP32 and sensor cases has detailed tips for creating functional enclosures.

Print settings for PETG:

  • Nozzle: 240°C | Bed: 80°C
  • Layer height: 0.2mm for walls, 0.12mm for the camera window area
  • 4 perimeter walls for strength
  • 15–20% infill (gyroid or grid)
  • Enable supports for button recess and screw holes

Step 5: Advanced Features to Add

Once the basic video doorbell is working, here are several upgrades to consider:

Motion Detection

Add motion detection to your ESPHome configuration so you get alerts even when someone walks past the door without ringing. ESPHome's built-in motion detection compares pixel changes between frames:

# Add to ESPHome config
switch:
  - platform: esp32_camera_web_server
    name: "Camera Web Server"
    port: 8080

binary_sensor:
  - platform: esp32_camera
    name: "Front Door Motion"
    id: motion_sensor
    update_interval: 500ms
    on_motion_detected:
      - mqtt.publish:
          topic: doorbell/front/motion
          payload: "ON"

Two-Way Audio

Add a small I2S microphone (like the INMP441) and speaker module (MAX98357 + small speaker) to the ESP32. ESPHome supports audio streaming, which lets you talk to visitors through your phone via Home Assistant.

Smart Lock Integration

Pair your doorbell with a DIY smart door lock so you can unlock the door directly from the doorbell notification. Create an automation that shows an "Unlock" button when the doorbell rings.

Night Vision

The ESP32-CAM has two built-in LEDs for IR illumination. Enable them in firmware and add an IR-cut filter to the camera lens for clear nighttime video. Swap the lens for one with a wider field of view (120°+) to capture the full porch area.

Troubleshooting Common Issues

Here are the most common problems you might encounter and how to fix them:

Issue Likely Cause Fix
ESP32 not flashing GPIO0 not connected to GND Check GPIO0 → GND jumper before powering
No camera stream PSRAM not enabled in config Add psram: enable to ESPHome YAML
Button press not detected Wiring or pull-up resistor issue Enable INPUT_PULLUP in config; check ground
Notifications not arriving MQTT topic mismatch Verify topic in ESPHome matches automation trigger
Camera disconnects randomly Wi-Fi signal too weak Add a Wi-Fi extender or move router closer

Securing Your Local Doorbell System

Since your doorbell is accessible from outside your home, security matters. Here are key measures:

  • Use a dedicated IoT VLAN — Isolate your doorbell and other smart devices on a separate network segment. Your main computer and phone network should be separate.
  • Enable HTTPS — Home Assistant supports Let's Encrypt certificates for encrypted remote access. Never expose the dashboard over plain HTTP.
  • Use strong passwords — Don't use default credentials for your Wi-Fi, MQTT broker, or Home Assistant login.
  • Keep firmware updated — ESPHome and Home Assistant both receive regular security updates. Enable automatic updates in the Supervisor panel.
  • Disable OTA after deployment — If you don't need over-the-air updates, disable the OTA feature in your ESPHome config to prevent unauthorized firmware pushes.

For complete home security coverage, combine your doorbell with a DIY home security system — motion sensors, door/window contact sensors, and sirens all integrate into the same Home Assistant dashboard.

Final Thoughts

Building a DIY smart doorbell with Raspberry Pi and Home Assistant is one of the most practical and rewarding maker projects you can tackle. For about the cost of a dinner out, you get a fully featured video doorbell that:

  • Streams live HD video from your front door
  • Sends instant push notifications with snapshots
  • Stores everything locally with no subscription
  • Integrates with your entire smart home ecosystem
  • Is completely customizable with 3D printed parts

The best part? You own every piece of it — the hardware, the software, and your data. No company can change your terms of service, raise your subscription price, or disable features you rely on.
