DIY Energy Monitoring System: ESP32 + Home Assistant Power Tracker (2026 Guide)

📅 August 13, 2026 ⏱️ 15 min read ✍️ 3D Print Starter Team
⚡

Your electricity bill keeps climbing, but you have no idea what's actually consuming all that power. A gaming PC running 24/7? The always-on server in the garage? The second refrigerator you forgot to clean out? A DIY energy monitoring system with ESP32 and Home Assistant answers all those questions — and it's surprisingly affordable to build.

In this guide, you'll learn how to assemble a complete energy monitoring setup for under $50, wire it safely to your main circuit panel, configure ESPHome firmware, and create a beautiful real-time dashboard in Home Assistant. You'll also see how to 3D print a clean enclosure to house everything on your workshop wall.

Why Build Your Own Energy Monitor?

Commercial energy monitors like the Sense or Curb cost $300-$500. You can build a DIY system that monitors your entire home's consumption, individual circuits, and even tracks costs per kilowatt-hour — for a fraction of that price. Beyond saving money, you get:

  • Real-time visibility into what's running and when
  • Anomaly detection — spot devices that start drawing unexpectedly high power
  • Cost tracking — see exactly how much each appliance costs per day, week, or month
  • Integration with Home Assistant — combine energy data with your existing smart home automations
  • Historical data — track consumption patterns over months and years

If you're already running Home Assistant (as covered in our Getting Started with Home Assistant on Raspberry Pi guide), adding energy monitoring is the next logical step to make your smart home truly intelligent.

What You Need: Hardware Components

The beauty of this project is that you only need a handful of components. Here's the complete shopping list:

Core Components

  • ESP32 Development Board — The brain of your energy monitor. Any ESP32 DevKit board works. We covered the Best ESP32 Development Boards in 2026 if you need recommendations.
  • PZEM-004T v3.0 Power Module — Measures voltage, current, power, and energy. Communicates via TTL serial. One unit handles up to 80A at 220V (or 100A at 110V).
  • SCT-013 Current Transformers (CT Clamps) — Non-invasive current sensors that clip around wires. Get 100A/50mA rated clamps for whole-house monitoring.
  • 3.5" TFT Display (optional) — For local readout without opening your phone. ILI9488 SPI displays work well with ESP32.
  • 5V Power Supply — A standard USB phone charger or a small AC-DC converter module.
  • Enclosure — You can 3D print one yourself. We have a guide on How to Design and 3D Print Custom ESP32 Cases for this exact project.

For Whole-House Monitoring

If you want to monitor your entire home's consumption (not just individual circuits), you'll also need a Split-Core Current Transformer (CT) that can clamp around the main service wires entering your breaker panel. These are rated for 100A or 200A depending on your panel's capacity.

Safety Warning: Working with Mains Voltage

This project involves working with 110V/220V mains electricity. Unless you are confident in your electrical skills, hire a licensed electrician to install the CT clamps on your main breaker panel. The CT clamps are non-invasive (they just clip around wires, no direct electrical connection), but you still need to be careful around the main panel.

For beginners, we recommend starting with individual circuit monitoring instead — plug a Kill A Watt meter into a specific appliance, or wire a PZEM module to a single outlet circuit you're comfortable working with. You can always expand to whole-house monitoring later.

Wiring the PZEM-004T Module

The PZEM-004T communicates with your ESP32 via a serial connection. Here's the standard wiring diagram:

  • PZEM TX → ESP32 GPIO 16 (RX2)
  • PZEM RX → ESP32 GPIO 17 (TX2)
  • PZEM VCC → ESP32 5V
  • PZEM GND → ESP32 GND
  • Current Transformer → PZEM Input (specific port varies by board version)

For the AC side, connect the live and neutral wires through the PZEM's power input terminals. The module has built-in voltage measurement, so you don't need a separate voltage sensor. Just wire it inline with the circuit you're monitoring.

ESPHome Configuration for Energy Monitoring

ESPHome is the easiest way to flash custom firmware onto your ESP32 for this project. We covered the ESPHome Getting Started guide in detail, but here's the key configuration for energy monitoring:

esphome:
  name: energy-monitor
  friendly_name: Home Energy Monitor

esp32:
  board: esp32dev
  framework:
    type: arduino

# Enable logging
logger:

# Enable Home Assistant API
api:
  encryption:
    key: "your-encryption-key-here"

ota:
  password: "your-ota-password"

wifi:
  ssid: "YourWiFiSSID"
  password: "YourWiFiPassword"

# UART connection to PZEM-004T v3
uart:
  id: uart_bus
  tx_pin: GPIO17
  rx_pin: GPIO16
  baud_rate: 9600

sensor:
  - platform: pzemac
    voltage:
      name: "PZEM Voltage"
      accuracy_decimals: 1
    current:
      name: "PZEM Current"
      accuracy_decimals: 2
    power:
      name: "PZEM Power"
      id: measured_power
      accuracy_decimals: 0
    frequency:
      name: "PZEM Frequency"
    apparent_power:
      name: "PZEM Apparent Power"
    reactive_power:
      name: "PZEM Reactive Power"
    power_factor:
      name: "PZEM Power Factor"
    energy:
      name: "PZEM Energy"
      id: total_energy
      unit_of_measurement: kWh
      accuracy_decimals: 3
      filters:
        - multiply: 0.001
    update_interval: 10s

# Utility sensor for daily/monthly energy tracking
utility_meter:
  energy_daily:
    source: total_energy
    name: "Daily Energy Consumption"
    cycle: daily
  energy_monthly:
    source: total_energy
    name: "Monthly Energy Consumption"
    cycle: monthly

This configuration reads voltage, current, real power, and cumulative energy from the PZEM module. The utility_meter sensors automatically reset on daily and monthly cycles — perfect for utility bill tracking.

Adding Multiple Circuit Monitors

One PZEM module monitors one circuit. For a comprehensive home energy setup, you can add multiple PZEM modules on the same ESP32 using a software serial approach, or use an ESP32 per major circuit group. Common monitored circuits include:

  • Whole house (main breaker)
  • Air conditioning / HVAC
  • Water heater
  • Kitchen outlets
  • Office / server room
  • EV charger (if you have one)

The beauty of spreading sensors across multiple ESP32s is fault isolation — if one board crashes, you don't lose monitoring on all circuits. For inspiration on multi-sensor ESP32 projects, check our guide on ESPHome Sensor Integration with DHT22, BME280, and Analog Sensors.

Home Assistant Energy Dashboard

Once ESPHome is sending data to Home Assistant, you need a dashboard to visualize it. Home Assistant 2024+ has a built-in Energy Dashboard that automatically graphs your consumption if you have the right sensors configured.

Configuring the Energy Dashboard

Navigate to Settings → Dashboards → Energy in Home Assistant. Add your utility meter sensors as data sources. Set your electricity rate (cost per kWh) and Home Assistant automatically calculates your daily, weekly, and monthly costs.

Creating a Custom Power Monitor Card

For a more detailed real-time view, use the Lovelace dashboard with a custom card like mini-graph-card or apexcharts-card. Here's a configuration example for a power monitoring card:

type: vertical-stack
cards:
  - type: statistic
    entity: sensor.pzem_power
    title: Current Power Draw
    unit: W
    stat_type: measurement
    period:
      rolling: 5

  - type: custom:apexcharts-card
    chart_type: area
    span:
      start: hour
    series:
      - entity: sensor.pzem_power
        name: Power (W)
        type: area
        color: orange
        fill_raw: last
        group_by:
          func: last
          duration: 1min

MQTT plays a crucial role in this setup too. If you want to integrate other smart home devices or build a more robust data pipeline, our guide on Setting Up an MQTT Broker for Smart Home (Mosquitto on Raspberry Pi) covers everything you need.

3D Printing the Enclosure

Once your circuit is working on a breadboard, it's time to clean things up with a custom 3D printed enclosure. This is where 3D printing and smart home projects intersect beautifully. You can design a wall-mountable case that holds the ESP32, PZEM module, and display in one tidy package.

We covered the fundamentals of designing custom cases for ESP32 projects in our ESP32 Case Design and 3D Printing Guide. For this energy monitor, design considerations include:

  • Ventilation — the PZEM module can get warm, so include ventilation slots or gaps
  • CT clamp routing — slots or channels to route the sensor wires cleanly
  • Display cutout — if adding a local screen, design a recessed bezel
  • Wall mounting — screw holes or a snap-fit bracket for the electrical panel
  • PLA vs PETG — use PETG for better heat resistance near electronics

Print settings for a functional enclosure: 20% infill, 3 perimeters, PETG filament, 0.2mm layer height. You can find printable enclosure designs on Thingiverse or design your own in Fusion 360 (see our Fusion 360 for 3D Printing Tutorial for the workflow).

Tracking Solar / PV Systems

If you have solar panels, you can extend this system to track both consumption and generation. The key addition is a second PZEM module on the solar inverter's AC output. Subtract generation from consumption to calculate your net energy usage and see when you're feeding power back to the grid.

Home Assistant's Energy Dashboard natively supports solar generation data — just add a second sensor for your PV output and configure it in the dashboard settings.

Automations Based on Energy Data

Now that your energy data is in Home Assistant, you can create powerful automations:

  • Alert when consumption spikes — if power exceeds 5kW for more than 5 minutes, send a notification
  • Turn off phantom loads — use a smart plug to cut power to a standby device when it drops below 2W for 30 minutes
  • Peak vs off-peak shifting — if you have time-of-use electricity rates, automatically run high-consumption devices during off-peak hours
  • Cost tracking notifications — daily summary of how much you spent on electricity that day

Troubleshooting Common Issues

PZEM readings are wrong or zero: Check your UART wiring (TX/RX aren't reversed). Verify the baud rate is 9600. Make sure the CT clamp is firmly clipped around the correct wire (not the ground wire).

ESP32 keeps crashing or rebooting: The PZEM can draw brief current spikes that reset the ESP32. Add a 1000µF capacitor across the 5V and GND rails near the ESP32, or use a separate 3.3V regulator for the PZEM's logic side.

Energy values not accumulating: The PZEM's energy counter resets when power is lost. If you need persistent energy tracking, use Home Assistant's utility_meter (which saves to disk) rather than relying solely on the PZEM's built-in energy reading.

MQTT disconnections: If you're using MQTT instead of the native Home Assistant API, see our guide on Tasmota MQTT Setup: Connect Your Devices to Home Assistant for troubleshooting connection stability.

Final Thoughts

A DIY energy monitoring system is one of the most satisfying smart home projects because it delivers immediate, tangible value. Within a week of monitoring, most people discover at least one device silently draining power — and cutting that waste often pays for the entire project's hardware within a few months.

The combination of ESP32, PZEM sensors, and Home Assistant gives you professional-grade monitoring at a hobbyist price. And with a 3D printed enclosure, it looks polished enough to install permanently in your home. Start with one circuit, get it working, then expand — the system grows with your needs.
