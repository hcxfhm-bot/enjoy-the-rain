Carbon Fiber Filament Guide: Settings, Tips & Best Uses

📅 May 22, 2026 ⏱️ 9 min read ✍️ 3D Print Starter Team
🛡️

Carbon fiber filament produces parts that look and feel industrial — matte grey finish, incredible stiffness, and dimensional stability that regular PLA can't match. But it's not a drop-in replacement. The chopped carbon fibers inside are literally abrasive, and they'll eat through a brass nozzle in as little as 500 grams of printing.

Here's everything you need to know before you buy your first spool.

What Is Carbon Fiber Filament?

Carbon fiber filament isn't pure carbon fiber — it's standard 3D printing filament (PLA, PETG, Nylon, etc.) mixed with 10–20% chopped carbon fiber strands. The fibers are short (0.1–0.3mm) and dispersed throughout the plastic.

The result is a composite material that's:

  • Stiffer — 2–3x the rigidity of base filament
  • More dimensionally stable — Less shrinkage and warping
  • Matte finish — Hides layer lines beautifully
  • Less prone to stringing — Fibers reduce oozing

The three most common types:

CF-PLA

PLA infused with carbon fiber. Easiest to print, lowest temperature, great surface finish. Best for display parts and structural components that won't see high heat. Temperature: 200–220°C nozzle, 50–60°C bed.

CF-PETG

PETG with carbon fiber. Better heat resistance and impact strength than CF-PLA. Slightly harder to print (more stringing). Temperature: 240–260°C nozzle, 70–80°C bed. See our PETG vs PLA comparison for base material differences.

CF-Nylon

The strongest and most heat-resistant option — and the hardest to print. Requires 260–290°C nozzle, 80–100°C bed, and ideally an enclosure. Warps aggressively if not contained. For serious functional parts only.

Benefits of Carbon Fiber Filament

  • Stiffness: CF-PLA is roughly 2x stiffer than regular PLA. Your brackets won't flex.
  • Dimensional accuracy: Carbon fibers reduce thermal expansion. Parts print closer to nominal dimensions.
  • Surface finish: The matte grey texture hides layer lines and looks premium.
  • Reduced warping: Even large prints stay flat (especially CF-PETG vs regular PETG).
  • Less stringing: The fibers thicken the melt, reducing oozing between moves.

Drawbacks (And They're Significant)

  • Abrasive: Will destroy brass nozzles. You must use a hardened steel or ruby nozzle. See our nozzle types guide for details.
  • Brittle: CF-PLA is more brittle than regular PLA. Thin parts snap easily.
  • Cost: $30–60 per spool vs $15–25 for regular filament.
  • Weaker layer adhesion: Fibers don't cross layer boundaries, so interlayer strength is lower.
  • Clogs: Fiber clumps can clog nozzles, especially at low temperatures.

Best Print Settings for CF-PLA

SettingValue
Nozzle Temp210–220°C
Bed Temp50–60°C
Print Speed40–60mm/s
RetractionSame as PLA (1–2mm direct, 4–6mm Bowden)
Cooling80–100% fan
Layer Height0.16–0.20mm (avoid ultra-thin layers)

Pro tip: Increase flow rate by 3–5%. The fibers make the filament slightly less viscous, so you need a touch more extrusion to fill gaps properly.

Do You Need a Hardened Steel Nozzle?

Yes. Period. Carbon fiber filament will wear a brass nozzle from 0.4mm to 0.5mm+ in as little as one spool. That means your prints get progressively wider, sloppier, and less accurate.

Your options:

  • Hardened steel nozzle ($10–15): The standard choice. Lasts indefinitely with CF filament. Slightly lower thermal conductivity means you may need +5–10°C on your nozzle temp.
  • Ruby nozzle ($35–50): Sapphire tip, brass body. Best thermal conductivity with CF-proof wear resistance. Expensive but the best.
  • Tungsten carbide ($30–40): Good middle ground. Better heat transfer than steel, nearly as wear-resistant.

For more on nozzle materials, see our 3D printer nozzle types guide.

What to Print with Carbon Fiber

  • Drone frames and propeller guards
  • Camera mounts and brackets
  • Robot chassis components
  • Gear housings and structural brackets
  • Tool handles and jigs
  • Automotive interior trim pieces

What NOT to Print with Carbon Fiber

  • Flexible parts (CF filament is stiff and brittle — use TPU instead)
  • Thin walls (will crack under load)
  • Parts requiring layer strength (carbon fibers don't bridge layers)
  • Anything food-safe (fibers can be exposed on surfaces)

The Bottom Line

Carbon fiber filament is worth the extra cost for functional parts that need stiffness and dimensional accuracy. Start with CF-PLA (easiest to print), upgrade to a hardened steel nozzle, and keep expectations realistic — it's stiffer but also more brittle. For purely decorative prints, stick with regular PLA and save the CF for parts that actually need it.
