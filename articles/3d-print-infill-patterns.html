3D Print Infill Patterns: Which One Should You Use?

📅 May 23, 2026 ⏱️ 6 min read ✍️ 3D Print Starter Team
🧵

When you slice a 3D model, you choose how much material fills the inside — and just as importantly, what shape that fill takes. The infill pattern affects print time, material usage, and how strong the final part is in different directions.

Most beginners just leave it on the default and never think about it. But switching your infill pattern can cut print time by 20% or double the strength of a functional part — without changing anything else.

Why Infill Pattern Matters

Infill isn't just "stuff inside your print." Different patterns distribute force differently:

  • 2D patterns (grid, lines, rectilinear) are strong in two directions but weak in the third.
  • 3D patterns (gyroid, cubic) resist force from every direction — better for parts under real stress.

Pattern choice also changes how fast your printer moves. Simpler paths = faster prints. Complex 3D paths = slower but stronger.

The 6 Most Common Infill Patterns

1. Grid (Lines)

The default in most slicers. Crisscrossing lines form squares, alternating direction each layer. Simple, fast, and good enough for most decorative prints.

  • Best for: Quick prototypes, display models, everyday prints
  • Weakness: No strength in the Z-axis. Layers can delaminate under vertical load
  • Speed: ⚡⚡⚡ Fast

2. Gyroid

An organic, wavy 3D structure that looks like brain coral. It's the strongest pattern per gram of filament — period. Gyroid distributes force equally in all three axes.

  • Best for: Functional parts, anything under load, parts that need to be strong in every direction
  • Weakness: Slightly slower to print than grid. The curved paths take more time.
  • Speed: ⚡⚡ Medium

3. Cubic

Stacked cubes rotated 45° each layer. A solid 3D pattern that's stronger than grid but not quite as efficient as gyroid.

  • Best for: Parts needing good all-around strength where gyroid isn't available
  • Weakness: More material than gyroid at the same density for slightly less strength
  • Speed: ⚡⚡ Medium

4. Triangles

Triangular grid pattern. Triangles are inherently rigid shapes, making this pattern stronger than grid in shear.

  • Best for: Parts under shear stress, structural brackets
  • Weakness: Still a 2D pattern — weak in Z
  • Speed: ⚡⚡⚡ Fast

5. Honeycomb

Hexagonal cells like a beehive. Popular for aesthetic reasons, and reasonably strong. Each cell distributes stress well within its layer.

  • Best for: Visible infill (transparent or translucent prints), moderate strength
  • Weakness: 2D pattern. Not as efficient as gyroid for pure strength.
  • Speed: ⚡⚡ Medium

6. Lightning

A Cura-exclusive pattern that only places infill where it's needed to support the top surfaces. Uses dramatically less material and time.

  • Best for: Busts, figurines, decorative prints where only the shell matters
  • Weakness: Almost zero internal strength. Don't use for functional parts.
  • Speed: ⚡⚡⚡⚡ Fastest

Infill Density vs. Pattern: Which Matters More?

Here's the thing most guides don't tell you: pattern matters more at low densities, density matters more at high densities.

At 10% infill, gyroid is roughly 40% stronger than grid. But at 50% infill, the difference drops to about 10% — there's so much material that the pattern shape barely matters.

Practical takeaway: If you want strength without weight, use gyroid at 15-20%. You'll get more strength than grid at 30% with less filament and faster print time.

When to Use Each Pattern

  • Decorative figurines: Lightning at 5-10%. Saves tons of time, looks the same on the outside.
  • Everyday prints (phone stands, cable holders): Grid at 15-20%. Simple and fast.
  • Mechanical parts (brackets, mounts): Gyroid at 20-30%. Strong in every direction.
  • Transparent or translucent prints: Honeycomb at 20%. Looks great through clear filament.
  • Flexible parts (TPU): Gyroid at 10-15%. Compresses evenly in all directions.

For more on how infill interacts with other settings like layer height and speed, check our complete settings guide and speed vs. quality breakdown.

Common Mistakes

  • Using 100% infill: Almost never necessary. 50% gyroid is stronger than 100% grid in real-world tests, and takes half the time.
  • Wrong pattern for flexible filament: Grid infill makes TPU prints stiff in one direction. Gyroid keeps flexibility uniform.
  • Ignoring the preview: Always check your slicer's preview to see how the infill fills the model. Some shapes leave large unsupported gaps at certain patterns.

If your prints are warping or the top surface looks rough, the issue might be too little infill supporting it. Our warping solutions guide covers this and other common fixes.

Conclusion

For most prints, gyroid at 15-20% is the best all-around choice — strong, efficient, and available in every major slicer. Use lightning for decorative-only prints, grid for quick everyday items, and bump density up to 30%+ only when you need real structural strength.

The right pattern can save you 20-40% on print time and filament without sacrificing any strength. It's one of the easiest wins in 3D printing.
