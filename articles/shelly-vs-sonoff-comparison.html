Shelly vs Sonoff: Which Budget Smart Home Platform Is Better in 2026?

📅 July 14, 2026 ⏱️ 12 min read ✍️ 3D Print Starter Team
⚡

If you've spent any time in the DIY smart home community, you've heard of both Shelly and Sonoff. They're the two most popular budget smart home platforms among Home Assistant enthusiasts, makers, and anyone who wants to automate their home without paying for an expensive proprietary ecosystem.

But which one should you choose? The answer isn't straightforward — both platforms have genuinely compelling strengths, and the "right" choice depends entirely on your specific situation: what you're trying to automate, whether you have existing smart devices, your budget, and how deep you want to go into DIY customization.

In this guide, we'll break down everything that matters: hardware, firmware, pricing, Home Assistant integration, ecosystem size, and real-world use cases. By the end, you'll know exactly which platform fits your project.

What Are Shelly and Sonoff?

Both Shelly and Sonoff are manufacturers of Wi-Fi-based smart home devices. They sell modules that go inside wall switch boxes, behind outlets, or inline with appliances — turning "dumb" electrical equipment into smart, app-controllable, automatable devices. Both run on ESP chips (Shelly on their own proprietary chips, Sonoff on Espressif ESP8266/ESP32), both are compatible with Home Assistant, and both support flashing custom firmware like Tasmota and ESPHome.

The similarities end there. The two brands take fundamentally different approaches to design philosophy, firmware, ecosystem, and pricing.

Shelly

Shelly is a Bulgarian company that launched in 2016. Their design philosophy centers on local-first, privacy-respecting smart home control. Every Shelly device has a built-in web interface, works without a cloud connection, and ships with scripting capabilities that most users never need to replace. Their hardware is consistently compact — a genuine advantage when retrofitting inside wall switch boxes. We covered Shelly's full product lineup in detail if you want a deeper dive into their devices.

Sonoff

Sonoff is a Chinese manufacturer owned by ITead Studio. They were one of the first companies to popularize affordable Wi-Fi smart home modules for the DIY community. Sonoff's strength is breadth of ecosystem — from $5 smart plugs to full Zigbee hubs with sensors, cameras, and more. Their stock firmware (eWeLink) is cloud-dependent, which is why the community strongly recommends flashing it with Tasmota or ESPHome for local control. Our Tasmota flashing guide walks you through the process step by step.

Hardware Comparison: Size, Build Quality, and Features

When it comes to hardware, Shelly and Sonoff take different approaches that have real implications for how and where you can install them.

Form Factor: Shelly Wins for Retrofitting

Shelly devices are remarkably compact. The Shelly Plus 1 measures just 39 x 36 x 17 mm — smaller than almost any competitor. This matters enormously when you're retrofitting a smart switch into a wall box that already has a neutral wire, a ground wire, and the original switch's wiring crammed inside. If you've ever tried to fit a Sonoff Basic into a European double-gang box, you know the struggle.

Sonoff devices are larger and come in more form factors. The Sonoff Basic (55 x 45 x 25 mm) is bulkier than the Shelly Plus 1, though Sonoff also sells compact options like the Sonoff Mini Extreme, which is closer to Shelly's size. Still, if you're working with tight wall box spaces, Shelly's compact design gives them a clear edge.

Power Monitoring: Built-In on Shelly

One of Shelly's most underappreciated advantages is that power monitoring comes standard on most of their devices. The Shelly Plus 1PM and Shelly Plus 2PM both include real-time power, voltage, and current readings — for only slightly more than the non-PM versions. Energy monitoring lets you:

  • Track appliance energy consumption in Home Assistant
  • Trigger automations based on power draw (e.g., "turn off the space heater when power drops below 50W")
  • Diagnose equipment health — a fridge drawing unusually high power can signal compressor problems

Sonoff charges a premium for power monitoring. Their Sonoff POW series (starting around $15–$20) is required for energy tracking, versus Sonoff's basic switches at $5–$8. If energy monitoring is important to you, Shelly's value proposition is significantly better — you get it included without a hardware upgrade.

Connectivity: Both Wi-Fi, Sonoff Has Zigbee

Both platforms operate primarily on Wi-Fi (2.4GHz). Sonoff differentiates with their Sonoff Zigbee lineup — a separate product range using the Zigbee protocol that includes hubs, contact sensors, temperature sensors, and smart bulbs. Zigbee's mesh networking means sensors can relay signals across your home, and Zigbee operates on a dedicated frequency that doesn't compete with your Wi-Fi.

If you want a Zigbee ecosystem, Sonoff's Zigbee hub is a compelling entry point — especially at $25–$30. Shelly has historically focused on Wi-Fi, though they offer Bluetooth on some devices and increasingly integrate with Matter for cross-platform compatibility.

Firmware and Local Control: The Critical Difference

This is where the philosophical divide between Shelly and Sonoff becomes most apparent — and it's the factor that matters most to serious DIY smart home enthusiasts.

Shelly: Local-First Out of the Box

Shelly devices ship with excellent firmware that prioritizes local control. Every Shelly device has a built-in web interface accessible over your local network — no cloud account required. Out of the box, you get:

  • Direct HTTP API access for local control
  • MQTT support for Home Assistant integration
  • CoIoT protocol for native Home Assistant discovery
  • Built-in scripting engine for device-level automations
  • Over-the-air firmware updates

For most users, there's no reason to flash Shelly devices with Tasmota or ESPHome. The stock firmware is already local-first, highly capable, and actively developed. This is a major advantage — you get enterprise-grade smart home functionality without touching custom firmware. If you do want ESPHome, community ports exist, though they require more effort than on Sonoff.

Sonoff: Better After Flashing

Sonoff's stock eWeLink firmware is cloud-dependent. Without flashing, your devices communicate through Sonoff's servers. This has real drawbacks:

  • Privacy: Your device data passes through Sonoff's cloud
  • Reliability: If Sonoff's servers go down or the company pivots, your devices may become unreliable
  • Latency: Cloud round-trips add delay compared to local control
  • Offline use: Without internet, eWeLink-controlled Sonoff devices are severely limited

The DIY community's standard advice for Sonoff is: flash Tasmota or ESPHome on day one. This swaps out the cloud firmware for a local-first alternative that gives you MQTT, HTTP API, and Home Assistant integration. Sonoff's hardware is solid — but you're leaving its potential on the table with stock firmware. Our Tasmota vs ESPHome comparison explains which firmware is right for different use cases.

Home Assistant Integration

Both platforms integrate well with Home Assistant, but the experience differs.

Shelly: Plug-and-Play via CoIoT

Home Assistant has a native Shelly integration that automatically discovers Shelly devices on your network via the CoIoT protocol. Once discovered, devices appear with switches, sensors, and power monitoring entities — no configuration files needed. For MQTT enthusiasts, Shelly also publishes clean MQTT topics that map directly to Home Assistant entities. The integration is mature, well-documented, and maintained by both Shelly and the community.

For those running Home Assistant on a Raspberry Pi (as we cover in our Home Assistant setup guide), Shelly devices are among the easiest to onboard. The combination of zero-configuration discovery and local MQTT makes for a smooth setup.

Sonoff: Works Well After Flashing

With Tasmota or ESPHome flashed onto Sonoff devices, integration is excellent. Tasmota devices auto-discover in Home Assistant with the Tasmota integration, and ESPHome devices generate beautifully formatted YAML configs with automatic entity creation. But out of the box, eWeLink's Home Assistant integration is cloud-dependent and less reliable.

If you're comfortable flashing firmware, Sonoff's integration quality after flashing is equal to — and in some cases more flexible than — Shelly's native integration. But there's an additional first step that Shelly doesn't require.

Pricing: Where the Real Difference Shows

Sonoff is cheaper on basic components. Here's a quick head-to-head:

Device Shelly Sonoff
Basic single-channel switch Shelly Plus 1: $15–18 Sonoff Basic: $5–8
Switch with power monitoring Shelly Plus 1PM: $18–22 Sonoff POW: $15–20
Dual-channel switch Shelly Plus 2PM: $25–30 Sonoff Dual R3: $18–22
Zigbee hub Not available Sonoff Zigbee Hub: $25–30
Temperature/humidity sensor Shelly Plus H&T: $30–35 Sonoff TH Origin + sensor: $15–20

Sonoff wins on pure price for basic switches. But Shelly's PM models (with power monitoring) often cost the same as Sonoff's non-monitoring versions — making Shelly's value proposition strong when energy tracking is part of your plan. Consider your total build cost, not just per-device price.

Ecosystem and Expandability

Sonoff has a broader ecosystem, especially if you want Zigbee sensors and cameras. Their Zigbee hub ($25–$30) opens up a whole range of battery-powered sensors — contact sensors, motion detectors, temperature sensors, and more — that are inexpensive and mesh-networked. For a new smart home builder wanting a complete system, Sonoff's ecosystem is more "ready to go" out of the box.

Shelly's ecosystem is more focused on in-wall devices and DIN-rail modules. They offer fewer sensor types out of the box, though their Plus Addon board lets you connect external sensors to Shelly Plus devices. For makers and DIY enthusiasts who want to build their own sensors using ESP32 and ESPHome, Shelly's flexibility at the hardware level is an advantage.

Real-World Use Cases: When to Choose Which

Here's how the decision breaks down in practice:

Choose Shelly when:

  • You're retrofitting smart switches into existing wall boxes with limited space
  • You want power monitoring included without paying a premium
  • You prefer plug-and-play local control without flashing firmware
  • You need the smallest possible form factor for a custom installation
  • You're building a DIY smart home using ESPHome and want maximum firmware flexibility

Choose Sonoff when:

  • You're building a Zigbee ecosystem from scratch on a tight budget
  • You want the lowest possible cost per switch
  • You're comfortable flashing Tasmota or ESPHome as part of your setup process
  • You want a broader range of device types (cameras, video doorbells, smart locks) from one brand
  • You're just starting out and want the most popular community-supported platform

The Best of Both Worlds: Using Shelly and Sonoff Together

Here's a well-kept secret in the DIY smart home community: you don't have to choose just one. Home Assistant makes it trivially easy to run both platforms under one unified dashboard. Many advanced smart home builds do exactly this:

  • Shelly Plus 1PM for in-wall light switches (compact, built-in power monitoring, works locally out of the box)
  • Sonoff Zigbee ecosystem for battery-powered sensors (door/window contacts, motion detectors, water leak sensors)
  • Sonoff POW for heavy appliances where you want energy monitoring (water heater, EV charger)
  • Both integrated into Home Assistant via MQTT for unified automations and dashboards

This hybrid approach gives you the best of each platform's strengths. Shelly wins where compactness and zero-config local control matter; Sonoff wins where Zigbee mesh networking and ultra-low-cost sensors make sense. Your Home Assistant dashboard won't know — or care — which brand a device is, and neither will you once everything is set up.

Verdict: Shelly vs Sonoff in 2026

Both platforms are excellent choices for a DIY smart home in 2026, and both integrate natively with Home Assistant. The decision framework is simple:

  • If you value hardware quality, compact design, and out-of-the-box local control — go with Shelly. Their devices are worth the premium if space is tight, power monitoring matters, or you don't want to flash firmware.
  • If you value lowest cost, Zigbee ecosystem breadth, and strong community support — go with Sonoff. Their Zigbee hub opens up an affordable world of sensors, and their community has spent years perfecting the Tasmota/ESPHome flashing process.

The smart home community's general consensus: Sonoff for budget-first builds, Shelly for quality-first builds. But the most sophisticated Home Assistant setups tend to use both — and that's exactly the right answer. Neither platform does everything perfectly, but together, they cover every base.
