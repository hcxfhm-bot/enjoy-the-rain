Nylon Filament Guide: Settings, Tips & Best Uses for 3D Printing

📅 May 24, 2026 ⏱️ 7 min read ✍️ 3D Print Starter Team
🧵

Nylon (PA) is one of the strongest and most versatile 3D printing filaments available. It's flexible, impact-resistant, and can survive conditions that would crack PLA or warp PETG. But nylon filament is also one of the hardest materials to print — it absorbs moisture from the air like a sponge, requires high temperatures, and needs an enclosure to print well. This guide covers everything you need to get nylon right.

Why Print with Nylon?

Nylon isn't for everyone. But if you need parts that take real abuse, it's hard to beat:

  • Impact resistance: Nylon absorbs energy instead of cracking. Drop a nylon part and it bounces; drop a PLA part and it shatters.
  • Flexibility: Nylon bends rather than breaks. Great for living hinges, clips, and gear parts.
  • Layer adhesion: When printed correctly, nylon layers fuse almost as strong as the material itself. Z-axis strength is far better than PLA.
  • Wear resistance: Nylon gears and bearings outlast plastic alternatives by 5–10×.
  • Chemical resistance: Resists oils, greases, and many solvents.

Types of Nylon Filament

  • PA6 (Nylon 6): Most common. Strong, flexible, high temp (240–260°C). Hardest to print — warps badly without enclosure.
  • PA12 (Nylon 12): Lower moisture absorption, easier to print, slightly less strong. Good starting point.
  • PA-CF (Carbon Fiber Nylon): Nylon mixed with chopped carbon fiber. Stiffer, less warping, beautiful matte finish. See our carbon fiber filament guide for details.
  • PA-GF (Glass Fiber Nylon): Similar benefits to PA-CF but with glass fiber. Easier on nozzles than carbon fiber.

Key rule: Keep cooling fan low. Nylon needs heat to bond layers properly. Too much cooling = weak layer adhesion and warping.

Bed Adhesion for Nylon

Nylon is notorious for poor bed adhesion. Here's what works:

  • PEI sheet + glue stick: Apply a thin layer of PVA glue stick to a heated PEI sheet. This is the most reliable method.
  • Garolite (G10) sheet: Nylon bonds well to garolite at 70°C. Popular in the community.
  • Painter's tape + glue: Budget option. Blue tape with a glue stick layer.
  • Brim: Always use a 5–10mm brim. Nylon's warp force is stronger than PLA — don't skip this.

For more adhesion tips that apply across materials, see our bed adhesion guide.

Drying Nylon Filament (Critical!)

Nylon absorbs moisture faster than any other common filament. Wet nylon hisses, pops, and produces stringy, weak prints with bubbles in the extrusion. You must dry it before every print if it's been exposed to air for more than a few hours.

  • Oven method: 80°C for 4–6 hours. Use a food dehydrator if your oven can't hold a stable low temp.
  • Filament dryer: Sunlu, Polymaker, or any dryer at 70–80°C for 6+ hours.
  • Storage: Keep in a sealed bag with desiccant. Print directly from a dryer if possible.

The full process is covered in our filament drying guide — the same methods work, just at higher temperatures for nylon.

Common Nylon Printing Problems

  • Warping: Use an enclosure and a brim. Pre-heat the enclosure to 45°C before starting.
  • Stringing: Dry the filament first. If still stringy, increase retraction by 0.5mm.
  • Weak layers: Reduce cooling fan. Increase nozzle temp by 5–10°C.
  • Bubbles/spitting: Filament is wet. Dry it immediately.
  • Nozzle wear: Pure nylon is soft on nozzles, but PA-CF will wear brass nozzles fast. Use hardened steel or ruby.

Best Nylon Filament Brands (2026)

  • Polymaker Polymide PA6-CF: Easiest carbon fiber nylon to print. Low warp, great bed adhesion.
  • eSun ePA-CF: Budget-friendly PA-CF. Good results, but dries slower.
  • MatterHackers NylonX: Carbon fiber reinforced, consistent quality, US-made.
  • Taulman Alloy 910: Pure nylon (PA6), extremely strong. Harder to print but worth it for functional parts.

See our full filament brands guide for more options across all materials.

Is Nylon Right for You?

Print nylon if you need functional, durable parts — gears, brackets, clips, drone frames, or anything that will be stressed or dropped. Skip it for decorative prints, display models, or if you don't have an enclosure and a filament dryer. The learning curve is real, but once you nail the settings, nylon opens up applications that no other filament can match.
