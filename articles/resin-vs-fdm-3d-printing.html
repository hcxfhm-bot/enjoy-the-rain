Resin vs FDM 3D Printing: Which Is Right for You?

📅 May 6, 2026 ⏱️ 10 min read ✍️ 3D Print Starter Team
🔬

The Two Main Paths in 3D Printing

Walk into any 3D printing forum and you'll see the same question pop up over and over: "Should I get a resin printer or an FDM printer?" It's not a trivial choice. The two technologies work on completely different principles, excel at different things, and demand different levels of commitment from you as a user. Pick the wrong one for your needs and you'll end up frustrated, out money, or staring at failed prints wondering where it all went wrong. This guide cuts through the noise and gives you a clear framework for making that decision — based on what you actually want to print, not on what's trending on YouTube.

How They Actually Work

FDM (Fused Deposition Modeling) — also called FFF — works like a hot glue gun on a robotic arm. A spool of filament (usually plastic) feeds into a heated nozzle, melts, and gets squeezed out layer by layer onto a build plate. It's mechanical, visible, and intuitive. You can watch the print happen in real time.

Resin (SLA/DLP/MSLA) uses an entirely different approach. A build plate dips into a vat of liquid photopolymer resin. A light source — laser in SLA, projector in DLP, LCD screen in MSLA — cures specific areas of that liquid into solid plastic, one layer at a time. The print hangs upside-down, growing out of the resin vat. It's chemical, not mechanical. And it's where most of the high-detail magic happens.

Print Quality: Resolution and Surface Finish

This is where resin absolutely wins, and it's not close. Resin printers routinely achieve layer heights of 25–100 microns (0.025–0.1mm), with surface quality that rivals injection-molded plastic. Miniature painters love resin for a reason — the layer lines are almost invisible, and details like chainmail or facial expressions come out crisp.

FDM printers, even good ones, typically print at 100–200 microns (0.1–0.2mm). You can push to 0.05mm on some machines, but that comes with trade-offs in print time and reliability. Layer lines are visible, especially on curved surfaces. Post-processing (sanding, priming) can hide them, but that's extra work.

Bottom line: If you're printing miniatures, jewelry, dental models, or anything where surface detail matters more than structural strength, resin is the clear choice. For functional parts, brackets, enclosures, or anything that needs to take abuse, FDM is more practical.

Materials and Strength

FDM wins on material variety. You can print in PLA, PETG, TPU, ABS, nylon, carbon fiber-filled blends, and more. Each has different properties — flexibility, heat resistance, impact strength. A well-tuned FDM print in PETG or ABS can survive drops that would shatter a resin print.

Resin materials are more limited and generally more brittle. Standard resins (the kind most people start with) are stiff and fragile. There are tough resins, flexible resins, and engineering-grade resins, but they're expensive and require different handling. UV exposure also affects resin prints over time — they can become more brittle if left in sunlight.

Property FDM Resin
Layer resolution 100–200 µm 25–100 µm
Material strength Good to excellent Brittle (varies by resin)
Build volume Large (200–300mm+ typical) Small (usually <150mm)
Post-processing Optional Required (wash, cure)

Ease of Use and Learning Curve

FDM is easier to live with. Filament comes on spools, stores easily, and doesn't require special handling. Prints pop off the bed and are ready to use. Failed prints are annoying but not hazardous. The learning curve is mostly about bed leveling, temperature tuning, and retraction settings — things you can pick up in a weekend.

Resin printing introduces chemicals into your home. The liquid resin is a skin irritant and potential sensitizer. You need gloves, ventilation, and a dedicated curing station (either sunlight or a UV chamber). After printing, parts need to be washed in isopropyl alcohol and cured under UV light before they're safe to handle. Failed prints mean dealing with uncured resin in the vat, which needs to be filtered or disposed of properly. It's not unmanageable, but it's not casual either.

Cost: Upfront and Ongoing

Entry-level resin printers are cheap — you can get a decent MSLA printer like the Anycubic Photon or Elegoo Mars for under $200. That's comparable to budget FDM printers like the Ender 3 V3 SE. But the ongoing costs stack up differently.

Resin is expensive. A liter of standard resin costs $25–$40, while a kilogram of PLA filament runs $15–$25 and prints much more volume (resin is denser). You'll also need isopropyl alcohol for washing, replacement FEP films for the vat, and UV curing equipment. Budget about $1–$2 per small print in resin vs. $0.20–$0.50 for the same volume in PLA.

Budget FDM printers also have larger build volumes, which means you can print more useful objects — phone stands, brackets, replacement parts — without worrying about hitting size limits.

Best Use Cases for Each

Choose FDM if you want:

  • Functional parts that need strength (brackets, mounts, tools)
  • Large prints (cosplay props, enclosures, furniture parts)
  • Low-cost material experimentation
  • A printer you can run in a bedroom without special ventilation

Choose Resin if you want:

  • High-detail miniatures (D&D, Warhammer, figurines)
  • Jewelry prototypes or dental models
  • Parts that need injection-mold-like surface finish
  • Small, intricate objects where detail matters more than strength

What About Both?

Many serious hobbyists end up with both. A budget FDM printer for functional prints and large projects, plus a resin printer for detailed work. That's the long-term path if you get deep into 3D printing — but it's not where most people should start. Pick one based on what you're most excited to print right now, master it, then expand. Trying to learn both technologies at once is a recipe for burnout.

Conclusion

If you're a complete beginner printing at home, FDM is the safer starting point. It's cleaner, cheaper to run, and more versatile. Resin makes sense if you already know you need that level of detail — if you're a miniature painter, jeweler, or dental professional, or if you've hit the resolution limits of FDM and want more. Neither is "better" in a vacuum. The right choice depends on what you're trying to make. Figure that out first, and the rest follows.
