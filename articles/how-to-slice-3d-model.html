How to Slice a 3D Model for Printing: A Beginner's Guide

📅 May 23, 2026 ⏱️ 7 min read ✍️ 3D Print Starter Team
💻

You downloaded a cool 3D model — now what? Before your printer can do anything, you need to slice that model into instructions the printer understands. Slicing converts your STL or 3MF file into G-code: the step-by-step commands that tell your printer where to move, how fast, and how much filament to extrude.

This beginner's guide walks you through every step of the slicing process. By the end, you'll know exactly how to take a model from download to print-ready G-code — without guessing at settings.

What Is Slicing, Exactly?

A slicer is software that cuts your 3D model into thin horizontal layers (hence the name). Each layer becomes a set of instructions: where to deposit filament, how fast to move, and what path to follow. The slicer also adds supports, infill, and other structures your print needs.

Think of it this way: The 3D model is the blueprint. The slicer is the translator. The G-code is the instruction manual your printer actually reads.

Popular slicers include Cura, PrusaSlicer, and Bambu Studio — all free. If you're not sure which one to pick, Cura is the most beginner-friendly.

Step 1: Import Your Model

Open your slicer and drag in your STL, OBJ, or 3MF file. The model appears on a virtual build plate. If it's way too big or tiny, most slicers auto-scale it, but always double-check the dimensions.

Quick tip: If the model is floating above the build plate, click "Drop to Build Plate" or "Place on Bed" — every slicer has this button. Prints must start from the build surface.

Step 2: Orient the Model

How you orient a model on the build plate dramatically affects print quality and strength. A few rules of thumb:

  • Flat side down: The largest flat surface should touch the build plate for better adhesion and fewer supports.
  • Minimize overhangs: Rotate to reduce areas that need supports. For more on this, see our overhang printing guide.
  • Layer direction matters: Parts are weakest between layers. If a part needs to bear load vertically, orient it so the stress doesn't pull layers apart.

Step 3: Choose Your Layer Height

Layer height is the single biggest factor in print quality vs. speed trade-off:

  • 0.12mm: Fine detail, slow print. Good for miniatures and display pieces.
  • 0.20mm: The default sweet spot. Good quality at reasonable speed.
  • 0.28mm: Fast and rough. Fine for functional parts where looks don't matter.

For a deeper dive, check out our layer height guide with real comparison photos.

Step 4: Set Infill

Infill is the internal structure of your print. You don't need 100% — almost no one prints solid. Common settings:

  • 10-15%: Light, fast prints. Fine for decorative objects.
  • 20%: The default for most prints. Good balance of strength and speed.
  • 50%+: Functional parts that need to handle stress or weight.

The infill pattern matters too. Grid and gyroid are the most popular — gyroid is stronger in every direction. See our full breakdown of infill patterns and when to use each one.

Step 5: Add Supports (If Needed)

Any area of your model that extends outward more than 45° from vertical needs support underneath. Your slicer can auto-generate supports — just toggle them on.

Support settings to know:

  • Support type: "Normal" (from build plate only) or "Tree" (organic branches that touch less of your model). Tree supports save material and are easier to remove.
  • Support density: 10-15% is usually enough. Higher density = harder to remove.
  • Support Z distance: Set 0.2mm gap between support and model surface for easier removal.

For the full support workflow, read our 3D print support settings guide and how to remove supports cleanly.

Step 6: Configure Print Temperature and Speed

Match your temperature to the filament you're using:

  • PLA: 200-210°C nozzle, 60°C bed
  • PETG: 230-245°C nozzle, 80°C bed
  • TPU: 210-230°C nozzle, 40-50°C bed

For speed, start with your slicer's default profile (usually 50-60mm/s for PLA). Once you're comfortable, you can push speed higher — our speed vs. quality guide shows you where the sweet spot is.

Step 7: Slice and Preview

Hit the Slice button. Your slicer generates the G-code and shows a layer-by-layer preview. Use this to:

  • Check for gaps or missing sections
  • Verify supports are where you need them
  • Estimate print time and filament usage

Always preview before printing. It takes 30 seconds and saves you from failed prints that waste hours of time and filament.

Step 8: Export and Print

Save the G-code file to your SD card, USB drive, or send it wirelessly through OctoPrint. Load it on your printer and start the job.

Common Slicing Mistakes to Avoid

  • Printing too fast on the first layer: Slow down to 20mm/s for layer one. Adhesion is everything. If your first layer isn't sticking, check our first layer fixes.
  • Skipping the preview: The preview catches 90% of problems before you waste filament.
  • Using default supports everywhere: Sometimes re-orienting the model eliminates the need for supports entirely.
  • Wrong filament profile: Make sure your slicer knows what filament you're using. PLA settings will fail badly on PETG.

Conclusion

Slicing is the bridge between a digital model and a physical print. Once you understand the basics — orientation, layer height, infill, supports, and temperature — you can reliably turn any STL into a successful print. Start with your slicer's default profile, preview every time, and adjust one setting at a time as you learn what each one does.
